<p class="lede">A hospital does not send every patient straight to its most senior surgeon. A triage nurse sees everyone first. Most people need a plaster or a prescription; a few need the specialist; and a doctor double-checks the cases where getting it wrong would be serious. Nobody thinks the answer to a crowded waiting room is “hire a more famous surgeon”. Yet that is exactly the reflex many teams have when their AI system is too expensive or makes too many mistakes: buy a bigger model.</p>

Before working on today’s large AI models, I built what is now called classic machine learning: a claims fraud-detection model at an insurer, workforce forecasting, and skill scoring for a labour-market dashboard. When generative AI arrived it was tempting to throw that toolbox away. That would have been a mistake.

My argument: **the cheapest, most reliable improvements to an AI system come from the statistics, simpler machine learning and human review wrapped around the model, not from swapping in a bigger model.** The bigger model sometimes helps, but it is usually the most expensive way to buy a small improvement. It also leaves the real problems in place: the system still can’t tell you which answers to trust, can’t prove its error rate, and can’t learn from the people who correct it.

## Why this matters now

Three things make the “boring” toolbox more valuable than ever.

- **Price differences are huge.** On Anthropic’s published prices, its top model, Claude Opus 5.5, costs $4 per million tokens of input (a token is a small chunk of text, often part of a word). Its small model, Haiku 5.5, starts at a fortieth of that price. Anthropic itself says the small model is built for jobs like sorting and routing. Sending every request to the flagship is a choice to overpay.
- **Trust is the bottleneck, and review habits are uneven.** In McKinsey’s March 2025 survey, 27% of organisations using generative AI reviewed all of its outputs before use, while a similar share checked 20% or less. Reviewing everything doesn’t scale; reviewing almost nothing is a gamble. What is missing is a principled way to decide *which* answers a person should see.
- **Risk teams want numbers, not adjectives.** “The model is usually right” won’t get past a risk committee. “The automated path is wrong 1.8% of the time, give or take 0.6 points, and we send the 12% of uncertain cases to a person” will.

## Layer 1: send easy work to the cheap model

Think of the system as a sandwich. The AI model is the filling. The statistics and simpler tools on either side are what make it safe to pick up. The first slice of bread is **routing**: deciding, before the expensive work starts, who should handle each request.

The research is striking. A 2024 study called RouteLLM trained routers that sent easy questions to a cheaper model and hard ones to GPT-4. On a chat benchmark it cut cost by more than 85% while keeping 95% of GPT-4’s quality. An earlier study, FrugalGPT, matched GPT-4 with up to 98% lower cost using a cascade. The savings were smaller on harder test sets, though: your mix of easy and hard questions, not the paper’s, decides what you save.

<Note kind="field">
In a hybrid chatbot I built, the “router” sat in front of an expensive document search. A smaller, faster model looked at the conversation and answered one question: does the conversation already contain what’s needed to answer this? If yes, the search was skipped. On conversations full of follow-up questions, that removed roughly 40% of the search cost. It wasn’t a new model, just a decision placed in front of an expensive step.
</Note>

## Layer 2: small specialists and honest confidence scores

For narrow sorting jobs with a fixed set of labels, small models trained on your own examples still beat giant models asked cold. Two 2024 studies found exactly that. The big AI model still has a starring role, just a different one: it is an excellent, cheap **labeller**. One study found ChatGPT labelled tweets more accurately than paid crowd workers on four of five tasks, at about a twentieth of the cost. So the recipe is: the big model labels, people check a sample, and a small model does the day-to-day work.

The second problem is confidence. When you ask a chat model how sure it is, you get text that *looks* like a probability but isn’t one. Research found that when models state their confidence, they tend to be overconfident, and that even the better confidence signals were only slightly better than a coin flip at spotting the model’s own mistakes.

The fix is **calibration**: take signals you can actually measure (how much several answers agree, how well the answer is backed by the documents it found), then check them against a set of cases where you already know the right answer, and adjust the scores until “80%” really means right 80% of the time.

**Conformal prediction** goes one step further and gives a guarantee. You hold back a few hundred to a thousand cases with known answers, see how wrong the model tends to be, and set a cut-off. Afterwards, for each new case, the system produces a shortlist that contains the right answer at least, say, 90% of the time. The size of that shortlist becomes a routing rule anyone can understand. One answer: automate it. Two or three: send it to a person with the shortlist ready. Empty or everything: something unusual is going on, so escalate. The guarantee holds only while new cases resemble the ones you tested on, so you keep re-checking.

## Layer 3: people where it matters, and arithmetic for the queue

Human review is the most expensive part of the system and the one most often designed by accident. Put people where the model is uncertain *and* a mistake would be costly. For confident, low-stakes work, automate it, but have someone review a small random sample, or you will never see the model’s *confident* mistakes.

Size the queue with arithmetic, not hope. Suppose 20,000 items arrive a day and 6% go to review: 1,200 items. At four minutes each, that is 80 hours of reviewing a day, or about 13 reviewers. Send 12% to review and you need about 27. The review threshold is a staffing decision as much as a statistical one.

Every human correction is also free training data. Feed it back into the router, the small models, the calibration and the tests, so each human decision makes the next automated one better.

The UK government’s Consult tool shows the sandwich in miniature. In October 2025 it analysed more than 50,000 public consultation responses in about two hours for £240, followed by 22 hours of expert checking.

## Layer 4: test scores need a margin of error

Every claim above rests on a test score, and most test scores in industry are reported with no “give or take”. That matters, because small tests are noisy.

## If you remember one thing

The AI model is the most impressive part of any system it sits in, and usually the least controllable. Everything around it, from the router and the small models to the confidence scores, the review queue and the tests, can be measured and improved, and all of it costs less than the next model tier. Before you buy a bigger model, build the instruments that show what the current one is doing. Usually they point to a cheaper fix. When they don’t, at least you will know the upgrade worked.
