<p class="lede">In February 2024, a Canadian tribunal ruled on an unusual defence. A passenger had relied on Air Canada's website chatbot, which told him he could claim a bereavement fare retroactively; another page on the same site said he could not. Air Canada argued, in effect, that the chatbot was a separate entity responsible for its own actions. The tribunal rejected that, held that the chatbot was part of Air Canada's website, and found the airline liable for negligent misrepresentation because it had not taken reasonable care to ensure the information was accurate. The sum involved was small. The principle was not: an organisation owns what its AI says, and "the model did it" is not a defence.</p>

Every organisation I work with now has an AI policy. Very few can answer, for any given output, three questions on the day something goes wrong: *Who asked? What did the system see? What did it say, and who acted on it?* That gap — between governance on paper and governance in the code path — is where most production AI risk lives, and it is the part engineers own.

My argument is this: **AI governance fails when it lives in policy documents and works only when it is encoded as controls in the request path that produce evidence an auditor can read.** A policy says what must be true. A control makes it true on every request. And evidence — logs, eval reports, access decisions, incident records — is the only thing that lets anyone, inside or outside the organisation, check that it was.

The article starts with the incident and breach data, which show where governance actually fails. It then sets out what the EU AI Act (including the 2026 omnibus changes), the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and ISO/IEC 42001 actually require of engineers. It proposes a control map of seven controls, goes deep on each — how to implement it and how to measure it — and looks at why agentic AI raises the stakes. After three cases, it takes on the strongest objections, sets out implications for boards, risk teams and engineers, and closes with a 30/60/90-day plan and the limits of the evidence.

### Policies are spreading faster than controls

The same data contain good news. Stanford's 2026 AI Index found that the share of organisations with no responsible-AI policies fell from 24% to 11%, and AI-specific governance roles grew 17%. McKinsey found organisations mitigating an average of four AI risks, up from two in 2022. Governance is no longer a niche concern.

But a policy is a statement of intent. The question that matters is whether intent reaches production, and three forces are making that harder. Usage is decentralising: anyone with a browser can use a model, so the perimeter of "our AI" now includes tools nobody approved. Systems are becoming compound: a single answer may involve a rewritten query, a retrieval step over permissioned documents, a model call to a vendor whose model can change, and a tool call that writes to a database. And autonomy is rising: agents act, rather than merely suggest. Each force widens the gap between what the policy says and what the system does.

## The evidence: where governance actually fails

### Breaches follow missing controls, not missing policies

IBM's 2025 Cost of a Data Breach study is the most practical governance data I know of, because it connects failures to specific missing controls. 13% of organisations reported breaches of AI models or applications, and of those, 97% lacked proper AI access controls. Among all breached organisations, 63% lacked an AI governance policy, and only 34% audited for unsanctioned AI. One in five reported a breach involving "shadow AI" — tools used without approval — which added about $670,000 to the average breach cost, against a global average breach cost of $4.44 million.

### Ownership is thin at the top, and review is thin at the bottom

In McKinsey's March 2025 survey, 28% of respondents said their CEO oversees AI governance and 17% said their board does; CEO oversight was among the attributes most correlated with bottom-line impact from generative AI. At the other end of the chain, 27% said their organisations review all generative AI output before it is used, while a similar share said they check 20% or less.

Both numbers point to the same problem. ## The regulatory landscape: what each framework actually asks of engineers

### The EU AI Act, after the omnibus

The EU AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024. Prohibited practices and AI-literacy duties applied from 2 February 2025; general-purpose AI (GPAI) and governance rules from 2 August 2025; and general application from 2 August 2026. In June 2026 the Council gave final approval to the "digital omnibus" simplification package, in force from 27 July 2026, which moved the deadlines that most organisations were worried about.

The omnibus also added a new prohibition on AI used to generate non-consensual intimate imagery or child sexual abuse material. The practical reading is simple: the delay buys time for high-risk *conformity*, not for the engineering that conformity will require. Several obligations already apply, and the high-risk ones that do not yet apply are mostly things you cannot retrofit in a quarter.

Read article by article, the Act is more of an engineering specification than many teams realise:

- **Article 4 (AI literacy, applies now):** providers and deployers must take measures to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy among staff who operate or use AI systems.
- **Article 50 (transparency, applies now):** people must be told when they are interacting with an AI system unless it is obvious; providers of systems generating synthetic audio, image, video or text must mark outputs as AI-generated in a machine-readable way where technically feasible; deployers must disclose deepfakes and AI-generated text published to inform the public, unless a human has editorial responsibility.
- **Article 12 (record-keeping, high-risk):** systems must technically allow automatic recording of events over their lifetime, sufficient to identify risks, support post-market monitoring and monitor operation.
- **Article 14 (human oversight, high-risk):** overseers must be able to understand the system's limits, stay aware of automation bias, interpret outputs, override them and stop the system.
- **Article 26 (deployers of high-risk systems):** assign oversight to people with competence and authority, monitor operation, keep system-generated logs for at least six months, inform affected workers and people, and report serious incidents.
- **Article 73 (serious incidents):** providers must report within 15 days of becoming aware, within 2 days for widespread infringements, and within 10 days where a death is involved — and must not alter the system in ways that would affect the investigation before informing authorities.

Every item on that list is either a log, a control, a user-interface element or a process with a clock. None of it is satisfied by a policy PDF.

### NIST and ISO: the voluntary frameworks that shape audits

The **NIST AI Risk Management Framework** (January 2023) is voluntary and organises risk management into four functions: Govern, Map, Measure and Manage. Its **Generative AI Profile, NIST AI 600-1** (July 2024), names 12 risks that are unique to or exacerbated by generative AI — including confabulation, data privacy, information security, harmful bias, human–AI configuration and value-chain and component integration — and maps suggested actions to each. NIST is revising the RMF under the US AI Action Plan, but the four functions are a stable vocabulary.

**ISO/IEC 42001** (December 2023) is different in kind: a certifiable AI management-system standard, in the same family as ISO 27001. Its Annex A is reported to list 38 controls across nine areas, from AI policy and impact assessment to the AI system life cycle, data and third-party relationships. As with 27001, an organisation selects applicable controls and justifies its choices in a Statement of Applicability, which is exactly the kind of artefact an auditor reads.

These frameworks are converging in practice. Stanford's 2026 survey asked which frameworks influence organisations' responsible-AI programmes.

## A framework: governance as a control plane

I think of AI governance as a control plane that sits in the request path between users and models. Every request passes through it; every control in it leaves evidence. Seven controls do most of the work.

The test for every control is the same: **if an auditor asked you to prove this control operated last Tuesday at 3 p.m., could you?** If the answer depends on someone's memory or a spreadsheet updated by hand, the control is a policy wearing a costume.

"Evidence an auditor can read" has three properties. It is **complete**: produced for every request, not sampled when someone remembers. It is **joinable**: the access decision, the trace, the model version and the eval report share identifiers, so a single output can be followed from request to release. And it is **trustworthy**: written by the system rather than by the people it governs, retained for a defined period and protected from quiet edits. Most organisations have fragments of all seven controls. What they usually lack is the joining — and without it, each fragment answers a question nobody asked.

## Deep dive 1: inventory and access — know what runs, and who runs it

### Mechanism

You cannot govern what you cannot see. The inventory is the foundation for every other control: it tells you which systems need which tier of logging, evaluation and oversight. In practice, a spreadsheet inventory decays within weeks. The inventory that stays accurate is the one enforced by architecture — a single AI gateway through which all model calls flow, where a call from an unregistered application is rejected. The registry then becomes a by-product of operation rather than a documentation exercise.

Access control is where IBM's data say the damage happens. In a retrieval-augmented system, access control has to apply in two places: who may call the system at all, and which documents the retrieval step may return *for this user*. A model that can retrieve the whole knowledge base will, sooner or later, summarise a document for someone who should not have seen it. The control is to filter retrieval by the caller's identity and group membership before anything reaches the model's context.

<Note kind="field">
On the enterprise RAG platform I led, every chat endpoint resolved the user through the company's identity provider, and retrieval was scoped by team-level groups so that sensitive knowledge bases were visible only to authorised staff. Because the access check sat in shared middleware rather than in each endpoint, a new endpoint inherited it by default instead of by someone remembering.
</Note>

### Failure mode

Shadow AI. IBM found one in five organisations had a breach involving unsanctioned AI and only 34% audit for it. The usual cause is not malice; it is friction. If the sanctioned route is slower or worse than a personal account, people route around it. A gateway that is fast, has good models and requires no paperwork for low-risk uses is a governance control disguised as a developer convenience.

### How to measure it

Three numbers: the share of AI traffic that flows through registered routes (estimate the denominator from network egress to known AI providers); the share of registered use cases with a named owner and a risk tier; and the share of requests with a resolved identity. Below 90% on the first, the inventory is fiction.

## Deep dive 2: quotas and traceability — limit the blast radius, keep the receipts

### Quotas, and the fail-open question

Quotas look like cost control, and they are; but they are also a security control. OWASP's 2025 Top 10 for LLM applications lists "unbounded consumption" alongside prompt injection and excessive agency. A per-user, per-application quota caps the damage from a compromised credential, a runaway agent loop or a scraping attempt.

The design decision that matters is what happens when the quota service itself fails. On the enterprise RAG platform, per-user monthly credit limits were enforced by a single decorator on every authenticated endpoint. A confirmed over-limit returned HTTP 429 with a structured body showing the limit, usage and remaining credit. But if the credit-tracking service was unavailable, the decorator logged a warning and let the request through — a deliberate fail-open choice, because for an internal knowledge assistant, blocking every employee because a billing service hiccupped was the worse outcome.

That was the right call for that risk tier, and it would be the wrong call for others. A system that can move money, change records or send external communications should fail closed: if the control cannot run, the action does not happen. The governance point is that the fail mode is a policy decision that must be written down per risk tier and implemented in code — not left to whatever the developer's exception handler happened to do.

```python
# Simplified sketch of the pattern: one decorator, fail mode set by risk tier
def governed(tier):
    def wrap(endpoint):
        async def inner(request, *args, **kwargs):
            user = resolve_identity(request)            # control 2: access
            try:
                if await over_quota(user, endpoint):    # control 3: quotas
                    return quota_exceeded(user)         # HTTP 429, logged
            except QuotaServiceError:
                log.warning("quota check unavailable", tier=tier)
                if tier.fail_closed:                    # high tier: no control, no action
                    return service_unavailable()
            with trace(user, endpoint) as t:            # control 4: traceability
                return await endpoint(request, *args, trace=t, **kwargs)
        return inner
    return wrap
```

### Traceability

Traceability is the control that answers "what did it see and say?" For a modern AI system, a useful trace links, under one ID: the user and their roles; the original and any rewritten query; the retrieved documents and their versions; the model and prompt version; the output; and any action taken. Article 12 of the AI Act requires high-risk systems to support exactly this kind of lifetime event logging, and Article 26 requires deployers to keep logs for at least six months.

Two design details matter. First, log the *rewritten* query, not just the user's message — retrieval quality problems and many injection attempts live in the rewrite. On the RAG platform, the query-rewrite step used tool calling, which returns schema-valid arguments and avoids the silent JSON-parsing failures that prompt-engineered output suffers in around 5% of zero-shot calls; every rewritten query was logged for audit. Second, generate stable reference IDs. In a triage prototype I built with Claude, every decision was appended to an audit log with a timestamp and a deterministic reference ID derived from the case text, so the same case submitted twice could be recognised. That was adequate for a prototype; production needs unique IDs, tamper-evident storage and a retention policy.

### Failure mode and measurement

The failure mode is logging that exists but cannot answer questions — free-text application logs with no shared ID, or traces that omit retrieval. A second failure mode is logging that creates its own privacy risk: traces contain personal data and must be access-controlled and retained no longer than necessary. Measure the share of requests with a complete trace, and run a quarterly drill: pick a random output from last month and time how long it takes to reconstruct it end to end. If the answer is days, the control is not working.

## Deep dive 3: release evals and drift — prove it was good, notice when it stops

### Release evals as a gate

An eval is the governance artefact that answers "is it accurate enough for this use?" with something other than a demo. The mechanism is a versioned evaluation set drawn from real use, a set of metrics with thresholds agreed with the use-case owner, and a pipeline that blocks release when a gated metric falls below threshold. The report is versioned with the release, so an auditor can see what was tested and what passed.

The hard part is scoring open-ended outputs at volume. On the LLMOps evaluation platform I built, evaluators were pluggable modules whose code lived in git while their prompts, weights and thresholds lived in a database, so the evaluation logic itself was version-controlled and its configuration was auditable. Several "judge" evaluators scored each output; an ensemble combined them with confidence weights; and when a human reviewer corrected a score, each judge's weight was updated through an exponential moving average (α = 0.2), so judges that agreed with humans gained influence gradually rather than from a single correction. After calibration, the ensemble reached a Spearman correlation of 0.89 with expert human raters on our evaluation data. The governance value is not the number; it is that every score is reproducible and every human correction is recorded and used.

### Drift

Approval is a snapshot; production is a film. Inputs drift as users find new uses, retrieval corpora change, and hosted models change underneath a stable name. Chen, Zaharia and Zou documented this for GPT-4: its accuracy on identifying prime versus composite numbers fell from 84% in March 2023 to 51% in June 2023, while GPT-3.5 moved in the opposite direction, and the June version was less willing to answer sensitive questions and made more formatting mistakes in generated code. If your eval passed in March and you never re-ran it, you do not know what you are running in June.

The control is continuous monitoring at three levels: input drift (are queries changing?), output drift (are answers changing?), and quality drift (are eval scores on a fixed canary set changing?). On the evaluation platform, a cheap check compared the centroid of recent answer embeddings with a frozen baseline using cosine similarity — linear in the embedding dimension, so it could run on every batch — and windows that crossed the threshold triggered a more expensive maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) test for confirmation. The centroid check misses changes in spread that leave the mean unchanged; that is an accepted trade-off for a first-line alarm.

### Failure mode and measurement

The failure mode for evals is the set that never changes: it measures what mattered at launch and slowly stops representing real use. Every incident should add at least one case to the eval set. For drift, the failure mode is alerts nobody owns. Measure time from a drift event to its detection, the share of drift alerts acknowledged by a named owner, and how often the canary set is re-run after a vendor model change — ideally automatically, on every version change.

## Deep dive 4: incident response — the control that proves the others

### Mechanism

Incident response is where governance becomes visible to outsiders. It has four parts: a **kill switch** that can disable a use case or route it to a fallback without a deployment; **rollback** to a known-good prompt, model and index version, which is only possible if those are versioned; an **escalation path** with named owners and a clock; and a **post-mortem** that produces changes to controls and eval sets, not just a document.

The clock is no longer just internal. Article 73 of the AI Act sets reporting deadlines for serious incidents involving high-risk systems — 15 days in general, 2 days for widespread infringements, 10 days where a death is involved — and prohibits altering the system in ways that would affect the investigation before informing authorities. That last requirement is an engineering constraint: you need to be able to contain an incident (kill switch, routing) without destroying the evidence (versions, traces).

### Failure mode

The failure mode is the incident nobody classifies as an incident. A chatbot gives a wrong policy answer; a support agent corrects it; nothing is recorded. Air Canada's case is the public version of that pattern. Most organisations have far more AI incidents than their incident log shows, because "the model was wrong" is treated as a support ticket rather than as a signal.

### How to measure it

Time to contain (from first report to kill switch or fallback); time to reconstruct (from report to a full trace of the affected outputs); the share of incidents producing a new eval case or control change; and the result of a twice-yearly drill in which a team must contain and reconstruct a simulated incident against the clock.

## Agentic AI raises the stakes

In practice, three controls change for agents. **Scopes** shrink: an agent gets the narrowest tool permissions that complete the task, granted per task rather than per agent. **Approvals** become structural: consequential tool calls go through an approval step that the agent cannot bypass, enforced in the tool layer rather than in the prompt. And **traces** extend to the whole plan: the sequence of tool calls, inputs and intermediate outputs, not just the final answer.

## Case examples

**Air Canada (2024): you own your AI's outputs.** A tribunal rejected the argument that a chatbot was a separate entity and held the airline liable for its negligent misrepresentation. The governance lesson is twofold. Customer-facing answers about policy must be grounded in, and traceable to, the authoritative source; and an organisation needs the traces to show what its system said and why. A system that answered from, and cited, the bereavement policy page — the one that contradicted the chatbot — would very likely have prevented the dispute.

**The Dutch tax administration (2021): data you should not have is a liability.** The Dutch Data Protection Authority fined the tax administration €2.75 million for processing applicants' dual nationality in an unlawful and discriminatory way when deciding whom to investigate for benefits fraud; the data should have been deleted years earlier. An inventory that covers features and data sources, not just models, plus retention enforced in code, is the control that would have surfaced it.

**GPT-4's 2023 drift: the vendor's model is not a constant.** Chen, Zaharia and Zou showed material behaviour changes in a hosted model within three months. The lesson is to pin model versions where providers allow it, re-run a canary eval set on every version change, and treat an unannounced behaviour change as an incident.

On the enterprise RAG platform I led, credit enforcement lives in one decorator applied to every authenticated endpoint. Changing the policy is a single code change that applies everywhere at once, and the 429 responses it produces are the evidence that it is working. That is what I mean by governance in the request path: the policy and its evidence change together.
</Note>

## Counterpoints

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 1: Governance slows innovation">
Every control adds latency, cost and process. Teams that wait for a registry entry, an eval suite and a risk tier before shipping will be overtaken by competitors who ship first and fix later. Heavy governance is a luxury for regulated incumbents.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
This is right about governance done as paperwork: review boards and approval forms do slow teams down, and the McKinsey data show organisations struggling to scale value even as they add policies. But controls in the request path are different. A gateway that handles identity, quotas and tracing is infrastructure that every team reuses; a new use case inherits it on day one. Evals are how you ship model upgrades quickly without fear. And Gartner's cancellation forecast suggests the bigger threat to agentic programmes is losing trust after an incident, not moving too slowly beforehand. The objection should change *how* you govern — tiered, automated, light for low-risk uses — not *whether*.
</Note>

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 2: Regulation will be delayed again, so wait">
The EU already pushed high-risk obligations back by more than a year. Standards are unfinished, guidance keeps changing and the US federal approach has shifted. Building to a moving target wastes money; better to wait until the rules settle.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
The objection is right that dates move and that building to the letter of an unfinished standard is wasteful. It is wrong about what is moving. Prohibitions, AI literacy, GPAI rules and Article 50 transparency already apply; liability for what your AI says, as Air Canada found, does not wait for any regulation; and IBM's breach data describe costs organisations are paying now. The controls in this article — inventory, access, tracing, evals, drift, incidents — are what every framework converges on (Exhibit 8). They are worth building for operational reasons alone, and they become your compliance evidence when the dates arrive. The work that is wise to defer is formal conformity assessment, not engineering.
</Note>

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 3: The model vendors handle safety">
Frontier providers invest heavily in safety, publish system cards, filter harmful content and offer enterprise terms. A company deploying their models should rely on that work rather than duplicate it.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
Vendors handle a real share of model-level safety, and you should use it. But almost every control in Exhibit 9 sits on your side of the API. The vendor does not know who your user is, which documents they may see, how much they should spend, what your eval thresholds are or when to call your regulator. The IBM breaches were overwhelmingly access-control failures — a deployer's responsibility. The AI Act assigns distinct obligations to deployers precisely because the provider cannot meet them. And the GPT-4 drift study shows that a vendor's model can change beneath you, which is a reason for your own evals, not for trust.
</Note>

## Implications by stakeholder

### Boards and executives

Ask for evidence, not assurances. Three requests will tell you more than any policy review: the AI inventory with owners and risk tiers; the percentage of AI traffic flowing through the governed gateway; and the most recent incident drill, with time to contain and time to reconstruct. Put AI governance under a named executive — the McKinsey data associate CEO-level oversight with impact — and fund the platform team that builds the controls as shared infrastructure, not as a tax on each project.

### Risk and compliance

Shift from documents to evidence. Map each obligation you care about — Article 12 logs, Article 14 oversight, Article 26 retention, Article 73 clocks, ISO 42001 Annex A controls — to a specific control and a specific artefact the system produces. Write fail modes per risk tier. Then sample the evidence: pick outputs at random and ask engineering to reconstruct them. Compliance teams that can read a trace are worth more than ones that can write a policy.

### Engineering and platform teams

Own the control plane. Build the gateway, the trace schema and the eval pipeline once, and make them the easiest path for every team. Treat prompts, retrieval indexes and model versions as versioned artefacts. Set fail-open or fail-closed explicitly. And make "what would an auditor need to see?" a design-review question, alongside latency and cost.

## Limitations and methodology

The evidence here has limits. Incident counts come from a database of publicly reported incidents, so they measure visibility as well as frequency. The McKinsey, Stanford and IBM figures are survey-based and largely self-reported; IBM's study covers breached organisations, so its percentages describe that population, not all firms, and its correlations between missing controls and breaches are not causal estimates. The McKinsey waves use different samples, and the agent figures mix two survey waves with a Gartner prediction that I read only in secondary reporting. My account of the EU AI Act is drawn from the Commission, the Council and an annotated version of the text, and simplifies obligations that depend on role and risk category; it is not legal advice. The ISO 42001 control count comes from a secondary summary because the standard is paywalled. The Chen, Zaharia and Zou drift result concerns one task on one model in 2023. Finally, the control map, the agent matrix and the 90-day plan are my own frameworks, drawn from systems I have built; the field notes describe those systems, and figures such as the 0.89 correlation apply to our evaluation data, not to evaluation in general.

## Governance is what the system does

The organisations that will handle the next few years well are not the ones with the longest AI policies. They are the ones that can pick any output from last month and show who asked, what the system saw, what it said, which version said it, what tests that version passed, and what happened when something went wrong. That is not a compliance aspiration; it is a set of controls that can be built in a quarter.

AI governance fails when it lives in policy documents and works only when it is encoded as controls in the request path that produce evidence an auditor can read. Write the policy, by all means. Then put it where the requests go.

<Note kind="insight">
Accountability is not a role on an org chart. It is the ability to reconstruct, for any output, who asked, what the system saw, what it said, and who was allowed to act on it. If your system cannot do that today, that is your governance roadmap.
</Note>
