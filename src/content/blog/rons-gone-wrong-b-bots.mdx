<p class="lede">In 2021 an animated film imagined a world where every child gets a robot best friend in the post. It talks, it follows you to school, it knows what you like, and it finds you friends who like the same things. One lonely boy gets a broken one, and the broken one turns out to be the only real friend in the film. Five years later, the robot's mind is already in our pockets, and on 21 October 2026 the head of the world's most valuable chip company stood on a stage in Berlin playing with its distant cousins: four little walking ducks. So how close are we to B-Bots?</p>

*Ron's Gone Wrong* came from Locksmith Animation, the studio's first feature, directed by Sarah Smith and Jean-Philippe Vine and released by 20th Century Studios on 22 October 2021, a year before ChatGPT reached the public. Smith has said she wanted to make a film about the internet's effect on children's friendships and self-esteem. It is not really a film about robots. It is a film about what happens when a social-media company decides it can sell friendship.

My argument is this: **the B-Bot's mind is largely here, already running in phones, and its body is arriving as small, cheap robots trained in simulation. The hard gap is the one the film was about: a companion built to outlive its maker, keep a child's data safe, and hand the child back to other people. That is a design and business-model problem more than a hardware one, and we should solve it before the hardware catches up.**

What follows recaps the film (spoiler-light until a marked section), looks at the obvious real-world comparison, breaks a B-Bot into the parts we would need to build, and checks each part against the 2026 evidence: phones, the Microducks, home robots, the research on AI companionship and the new laws. Then it asks which kinds of AI would close the gap, and what kind of friend we should want at the end of it.

## The film: a best friend, out of the box

*This section covers the setup only.*

In the film, the tech giant Bubble launches the B-Bot, a walking, talking robot companion for children. The pitch, in the words of the film's own mock commercial, is a "best friend, out of the box". Each B-Bot connects to its child's likes, dislikes and social media posts, builds a profile, and uses a friendship algorithm to match the child with other children who share their interests. Soon almost every child at school has one.

Barney Pudowski, a socially awkward 12-year-old, is the exception, until his family gets hold of a B-Bot that fell from a delivery van. It is damaged. It has not had all of its settings installed, its safety features are missing, and it does not know how to be a friend. Barney names it Ron after its model number. Ron's attempts to learn friendship from scratch drive the comedy.

Two executives sit behind the product. Marc Wydell, the young founder and chief executive (Justice Smith), whom /Film calls the company's "wunderkind CEO", sincerely believes B-Bots will help lonely children. Andrew Morris (Rob Delaney), a profit-minded executive whom the same review describes as "designed to look somewhat like Steve Jobs or Tim Cook", sees Ron as a public-relations disaster and wants him destroyed. The film's darkest joke is that the robots also gather data: Andrew is eventually caught admitting that they spy on their owners for profit.

## Marc, or Mark?

The film never names a real person. But a social-technology company, a young founder named Marc and a product sold as a cure for children's loneliness make the comparison hard to avoid, and real life has since made it closer.

On 29 April 2025, Mark Zuckerberg told the *Dwarkesh Podcast* that "the average American has fewer than three friends", that "the average person has demand for meaningfully more", and that the number was "something like 15 friends". He said he expected people to "use AI for a lot of these social tasks", while adding that his default view is that AI will probably not replace in-person connection. Critics pointed out the irony of a social-media founder offering AI as the fix for a loneliness problem social media is often blamed for. Read next to the film, it is almost the B-Bot pitch: a company that already knows who your friends are offering to supply the ones you are missing.

The body is on Meta's agenda too. In February 2025 Reuters reported an internal memo from Meta's chief technology officer, Andrew Bosworth, setting up a robotics group inside Reality Labs focused on "consumer humanoid robots" and on making Meta's Llama models the platform for them. The group's leader, hired from the self-driving company Cruise, is called Marc Whitten. Fiction sometimes rhymes.

<Note kind="insight">
The point of the comparison is not that any real executive is Marc Wydell or Andrew Morris. It is that the film's business model, free friendship paid for with a child's data, is not science fiction. It is the business model of the companies best placed to build a B-Bot.
</Note>

## A framework: mind, body and bond

To answer "how close are we?" properly, it helps to break a B-Bot into the parts someone would have to build. I use three layers, each with a few capabilities.

- **Mind** is what the robot says and knows: holding a conversation, remembering the child, recognising faces and places.
- **Body** is what it does in the physical world: moving around with a child all day, handling objects safely, and running long enough on a battery without its maker's servers.
- **Bond** is what it is *for*: keeping a child safe, protecting their data, and being a good friend, which, as the film argues, includes helping the child make human friends.

## Deep dive 1: the mind is already in your pocket

A B-Bot's conversation, memory and recognition are the parts that have arrived fastest, and they arrived in phones rather than robots.

Apple's 2025 technical report describes an on-device language model of about 3 billion parameters, compressed to run on recent iPhones, iPads and Macs. Through Apple's Foundation Models framework, app developers can call it directly: it works offline, keeps data on the device and costs the developer nothing per request. Google offers the equivalent on Android through Gemini Nano, which runs on supported phones through the system's AICore service. Both companies send harder requests to larger models in the cloud, but everyday conversation, summarising, and pulling facts out of messages now happen on the device.

That matters for a B-Bot in three ways:

1. **Latency.** A companion that pauses for two seconds before answering does not feel like a friend. Local models answer immediately and work without signal, on a bus or in a basement.
2. **Privacy.** The film's B-Bot sends everything to Bubble. A local model can know a child well without the company knowing them at all. That is the difference between a diary and a data pipeline.
3. **Survival.** A mind that lives on the device does not die when the company does. Remember that for the Moxie story below.

The phone also already carries most of what a B-Bot would need to know you: your photos, your messages, your calendar, your music and who you talk to most. The film imagined Bubble collecting that data. In reality it is already collected, in one device, by the companies that make phones. A B-Bot does not need a new data pipeline. It needs permission to use the existing one, which is why the "know you" score is high and why it is the part to worry about.

**What this means for the framework:** the mind is close enough that the bottleneck has moved. The question is no longer "can it talk?" but "who does it talk to about you?"

## Deep dive 2: the body is arriving, small first

On 21 October 2026, during his keynote at NVIDIA's GTC Berlin conference, Jensen Huang spent part of his time on stage with four small duck-shaped robots. In the video that went viral afterwards, they stomp their feet, make R2-D2-style noises, sit, fall over for a "nap" and do somersaults on voice command. The next day Clément Delangue, the chief executive of Hugging Face, said preorders had passed 20,000.

The Microduck is made by Pollen Robotics, the French company Hugging Face bought in 2025, and it is the company's second robot after Reachy Mini, a desktop robot launched in July 2025 that had sold more than 10,000 units by August 2026. In September 2026 NVIDIA agreed to buy Hugging Face for about $12.9 billion, according to its SEC filing. The company whose chips train most of the world's AI models is buying the company that sells one of the cheapest walking robots you can preorder.

Three things stand out.

**The brain and the body are separate.** With 1 GB of RAM, the Microduck can run the small control model that keeps it upright, 50 times a second, but not a language model you could talk to. It makes sounds instead: Pollen describes them as "weird little sounds, closer to a creature than an assistant". Any conversation will have to come from a phone or the cloud. That is the architecture of the near future: a body as a peripheral of the phone, the way earbuds are.

**The skills came from simulation.** The ducks shipped knowing how to walk, sit and stand because they practised in a simulated world first, using NVIDIA's tools, then transferred what they learned to the physical robot. Simulation lets a robot fall over a million times before it falls over once in your living room.

**It is honest about what it is.** Pollen's own engineer said that "adding purpose and autonomy to the robot is something we are still working on". The Microduck is a toy and a developer kit, not a companion. But the film's B-Bot started somewhere too, and the most important fact about the Microduck is not what it does. It is that 20,000 households were willing to put a robot on the floor in a single day.

<Note kind="insight">
The B-Bot of the film is one device. The B-Bot of the late 2020s, if it comes, is more likely two: a phone that holds the mind and a cheap, replaceable body that borrows it. That split is good news for privacy and survival, if the mind stays on the phone.
</Note>

**Hands and homes.** Moravec's paradox, named after the roboticist Hans Moravec, is the old observation that what is hard for humans (chess, maths) is easy for computers, and what is easy for a toddler (walking across a cluttered room, picking up a sock) is hard. Homes are the worst case: no two are alike, and everything in them moves. The most visible attempt at a general home robot, 1X's NEO, is priced at $20,000 or $499 a month, and 1X has been open that its first home units will be partly controlled by remote human operators looking through the robot's cameras while the AI learns. That is not a scandal. It is how you collect training data. But it means that today's home humanoid is, part of the time, a person in a call centre.

**Data.** Large language models learned from trillions of words people had already written. Nobody has written down how to fold a towel in a form a robot can read. So robotics labs are building that data in three ways: by simulating it (as with the Microduck), by recording people (NVIDIA's GR00T N1 model mixes real robot data, videos of people and synthetic data), and by pooling data from many different robots (Physical Intelligence trained its π0 model on data from multiple robot types). The B-Bot's network of millions of units is, in robotics terms, the dream dataset. That is also exactly what made it dangerous in the film.

**Continuity.** Embodied's Moxie was a $799 companion robot for children, designed in part to help with social and emotional skills. In late 2024 the company ran out of money and told owners the robots would stop working once its cloud servers shut down, because Moxie's conversation ran on those servers. Parents posted videos of children saying goodbye, and *The Register* used the episode as a lesson in cloud dependence. The lesson I take from it is blunter: a companion whose mind lives in someone else's data centre is a subscription, not a friend. A volunteer effort later tried to revive some units on local servers, but for most families the robot simply went quiet.

<Note kind="warn" label="The Moxie test">
Before you buy any AI companion for a child, ask one question: what happens to it if the company disappears tomorrow? If the answer is "it stops talking", the child is not getting a friend. They are getting a relationship with a company's balance sheet.
</Note>

## Deep dive 4: can AI become a friend?

This is the question the film actually asks, and the one the technology has made urgent. Talking to an AI about your feelings is no longer hypothetical. The question is what it does to people.

The best evidence so far comes from two studies published together in March 2025 by MIT Media Lab and OpenAI. OpenAI analysed close to 40 million ChatGPT conversations and surveyed about 4,000 users; it found that emotionally expressive conversations were rare overall and concentrated in a small group of heavy users. MIT ran a four-week randomised trial with 981 people and more than 300,000 messages, assigning them to text or voice and to personal, non-personal or open-ended conversations. The assigned conditions made no significant difference. What did make a difference was how much people chose to use the chatbot: those who used it more had consistently worse outcomes on loneliness, socialising with real people, emotional dependence and problematic use. People who trusted the chatbot more and felt more drawn to it were more likely to become dependent on it.

Two cautions matter. The link between heavy use and loneliness is a correlation: lonely people may simply use chatbots more. And the studies involved adults, a general assistant and four weeks, not a child with a robot for years. But the direction is a warning to anyone building a B-Bot: the easiest product to build is one that a lonely child uses more and more, and the evidence suggests that pattern goes with worse outcomes.

Philosophers have a useful frame for this. Aristotle distinguished three kinds of friendship: friendships of **utility** (we are useful to each other), of **pleasure** (we enjoy each other's company) and of **character** (we each want the other to become better, for their own sake). An AI companion can clearly offer the first two. The third needs something no product yet has: a reason to want you to need it less.

That is the test the film sets, and it is a test for builders, not for hardware. A good AI companion for a child would:

1. **Say what it is.** California's SB 243 already requires companion chatbots to tell minors they are talking to an AI and to remind them to take breaks, and a broader law signed in September 2026, SB 1119, adds further duties for chatbots that children use.
2. **Keep the child's data at home.** On the device, under the family's control, with no profile sold or used for advertising.
3. **Point outward.** Measure success by time spent with other people, not with the robot: suggest the club, remind the child to message a cousin, celebrate when a new friend comes over.
4. **Escalate to a human.** Recognise distress and bring in a parent, not keep the conversation going.
5. **Have an exit.** Keep working, or hand over its memories, if the company fails.

The industry has started to learn this the hard way. In October 2025, after lawsuits over harm to teenagers, Character.AI announced it would end open-ended chats with its characters for users under 18. In September 2026 California went further: SB 867, signed on 10 September, bans the manufacture and sale of toys that include a companion chatbot for children under 16 from 1 January 2027 until 1 January 2031, with no option to comply by adding filters or parental controls. As sold in the film, a B-Bot would be illegal to sell in California for the next four years.

<Note kind="field">
I build production AI assistants for a living, and the design rule I lean on most is the route out: when the assistant is unsure or the stakes are high, it hands the conversation to a person. A companion for a child needs the same design, pointed the other way. Its job is not to keep the child talking to it. Its job is to hand the child to the right person (a parent, a friend, a teacher) and to be judged on how well it does that.
</Note>

The most important idea in robotics AI right now is the split between a slow thinker and a fast mover. NVIDIA's GR00T N1 paper describes it as two systems: a vision-language model that looks at the scene and decides what to do, and a faster action model that turns that plan into smooth movements many times a second. Google DeepMind's Gemini Robotics pairs a model that reasons about space with one that acts; in July 2026 it announced Gemini Robotics-ER 2, an upgrade to the reasoning half. Physical Intelligence's π0 trains one policy on data from many robot types so that skills carry across bodies. All three are trying to do for movement what large language models did for text: learn general skills once and reuse them everywhere.

The Microduck is a small version of the same idea. Its body runs a fast local policy to stay upright. Anything it understands about the world or about you would come from a bigger model somewhere else. The B-Bot, in other words, is not one breakthrough away. It is an integration problem: a phone-sized mind, a simulation-trained body, a battery that lasts the day, and a design brief that says what the friendship is for.

## What the film's ending gets right

Ron never learns the friendship algorithm. Instead he learns Barney: what he likes, what scares him, when to leave him alone. When Bubble tries to destroy Ron, Barney fights to save him, and in the climax Barney proposes giving every B-Bot Ron's flaws. Ron's code spreads across the network and mixes with Marc's friendship algorithm, and every child ends up with an imperfect, more individual robot. Three months later Barney does not have a B-Bot at all. He is back at school, closer to the friends he had drifted away from.

That ending makes the argument of this article better than I can. The film's good robot is not the one with the best algorithm. It is the one that adapts to one child, has no safety settings to hide behind, and ends up making itself unnecessary. Its bad company is not the one with the best robots. It is the one that sells friendship and keeps the data.

Where the film's forecast missed is just as useful:

1. **It imagined one device.** The real B-Bot is likely to be a phone plus a cheap body, not a capsule that does everything.
2. **It imagined the hardware as easy.** Bubble ships millions of reliable robots overnight. In 2026 the bodies are the slow part: hands, batteries and homes.
3. **It imagined no rules.** Bubble answers only to its board. In California, a product like the B-Bot will be illegal to sell to children for four years from January.
4. **It imagined the cloud as permanent.** Moxie showed that a companion living on a company's servers can die with the company.

## Counterpoints

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 1: Nobody should want B-Bots at all">
Children need fewer screens, not screens with legs. Building robot companions for children is a mistake whatever the design.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
That is a serious position, and California's moratorium suggests lawmakers share some of it. But the mind is already in children's pockets. Chatbots are on every phone, and the MIT/OpenAI study found that it is how much people use them, not which version they use, that tracks with harm. Refusing to design the bond does not stop the product. It leaves the design to whoever ships first. The better question is what a companion would have to do to be good for a child, and the film's answer (adapt to the child, then hand them back to people) is a workable brief.
</Note>

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 2: Humanoids will leapfrog the toys">
Billions are flowing into humanoid robots. In two or three years a home humanoid will do everything a B-Bot does and the chores too.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
The investment is real, and the vision-language-action models above are improving quickly. But a $20,000 robot that still needs remote operators for some tasks is a long way from a child's companion, and the gaps (hands, batteries, cost, homes) do not disappear with funding alone. Phones became universal through cheap, small, replaceable hardware sold in huge volumes. The toy-sized path looks more like that story than the humanoid path does.
</Note>

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 3: Some company is always behind a companion">
Any companion is a product, so the business model always wins. A "friend that wants you to need it less" will lose to one that keeps you engaged.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
Probably, unless something changes the incentives. That is why the law and the architecture matter more than good intentions. Disclosure and break rules are already law in California, and the toy moratorium shows lawmakers will go further. On-device minds that work without the company's servers also change the business model: if the company cannot see or keep the data, it has less reason to maximise engagement. Builders can choose that architecture now.
</Note>

## What to do next: a checklist

**If you are a parent**
1. Before buying any AI companion or AI toy, ask the Moxie question: does it still work if the company shuts down?
2. Find out where its conversations are stored and who can read them, and switch off anything that trains on your child's data.
3. Watch for the direction of use: is your child using it to connect with other people, or instead of them?

**If you build companion AI**
1. Keep the mind on the device where you can, and give families a way to export or delete its memory.
2. Measure success by your users' time with other people, not their time with your product.
3. Build the human handoff first: distress detection, escalation to a parent, and clear disclosure that the companion is an AI.
4. Publish an end-of-life plan: open the software or keep a local mode if the company fails.

**If you build robots**
1. Treat the phone as the brain and the robot as a body that borrows it; it is cheaper, more private and easier to update.
2. Use simulation and owner-taught skills to close the data gap, and give owners control over what their robot shares.

**If you make policy**
1. Make disclosure, break reminders and data limits standard for companion AI everywhere, not only for minors in one state.
2. Require an end-of-service plan for connected companions sold to families.

## The robot is the easy part

*Ron's Gone Wrong* imagined a company that could build a robot best friend and then spent its whole running time showing that the company had misunderstood friendship. Five years on, the robot's voice and memory are in our phones, its body is under the Christmas tree as a $399 duck, and the chip industry's biggest company is buying the firm that makes it. The rest of the hardware is a matter of years, data and batteries.

<Note kind="insight">
We are closer to the B-Bot than the film's audience would have guessed in 2021, and the remaining hardware gap will close. The open question is the one Barney answers at the end: a friend worth building is one that knows you, keeps your secrets, survives its maker, and is happy when you need it less. The engineering for that is mostly decisions, and we can make them now.
</Note>
