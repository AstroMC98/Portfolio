<p class="lede">In 2019 an anime imagined a Mars where machines write the hits and almost nobody minds. Two teenagers, a refugee orphan with a keyboard and a runaway with a guitar, start writing their own songs anyway, and the story treats that as a quiet act of rebellion. Seven years later, back on Earth, one streaming service receives nearly 90,000 fully AI-generated tracks every day. <em>Carole & Tuesday</em> did not predict the technology. It predicted the argument.</p>

The show came from Studio Bones and supervising director Shinichirō Watanabe, the director of *Cowboy Bebop*, and it aired before ChatGPT, Suno or Udio had reached the public. Its Mars is a place where artificial intelligence produces most culture and people mostly consume it. The machine-made pop in the show is polished, popular and slightly airless. What it lacks is harder to name, and the series spends 24 episodes trying to name it.

My argument is this: **the show was right that AI would make competent, chart-ready music at industrial scale, and right about what that leaves scarce: a life behind the song, a stage, trust, and someone who answers for the work. The fights of 2026 over training consent, disclosure, voice cloning, royalty dilution and discovery are the ones the show kept in the background, and they will decide whether that scarcity is rewarded or exploited.**

What follows recaps what the show imagined (spoiler-light), offers a simple model of what AI can and cannot copy, and tests it against the 2025–26 evidence on uploads, listeners, live music and the law, including two viral Filipino songs. The show's back half comes later, behind a spoiler warning.

## Context: why this matters now

When an interviewer from *Looper* asked Watanabe about AI art programs, which had arrived in the news after the show was made, he answered: "The future that I imagined has arrived." He added that the images such programs made were often strange and distorted, so he thought the full vision was still a long way off. Music has moved faster than his caution.

The numbers now have the shape of the show's premise. Suno, the best-known AI song generator, was valued at $5.4 billion after raising more than $400 million in June 2026, and Deezer says AI tracks are more than half of its deliveries on peak days.

## The show: a Mars where machines write the hits

*This section covers the setup and the first half of the series only.*

The official synopsis sets the story about fifty years after humanity began moving to Mars, in an era when AI produces most culture and people are mostly its consumers. Carole is a part-time worker in Alba City who wants to be a musician. Tuesday comes from a wealthy family in Herschel City and wants the same thing, with nobody at home who understands it. They meet by chance on a bridge and decide to make music together.

The two are drawn as opposites. *Anime News Network*'s reviewer described Carole as an orphaned refugee from Earth who busks with a keyboard between part-time jobs, and Tuesday as a politician's daughter who runs from her privileged life with an acoustic Gibson guitar. Around them gather a washed-up former rocker, Gus, who appoints himself their manager, and a young sound engineer, Roddy, whose video of them goes viral.

The machine side of the world arrives in the first episode. Angela, a former child actress and model, is taken on as a singer by Tao, a producer who uses advanced AI to build high-selling performers. The ANN review describes Tao using AI and mechanical enhancement to shape her, so that algorithms decide every syllable she sings. A star DJ, Ertegun, scoffs at the very idea of music not made by AI. A cheap robot video director the duo hire turns out to be a scam. And the first half builds towards *Mars' Brightest*, a televised talent show where the duo's self-written songs and Angela's AI-shaped act compete for the same prize.

Two details make the show a good lens for this argument. First, its episodes are named after real songs: "True Colors", "Born to Run", "Fire and Rain", "Video Killed the Radio Star", "Every Breath You Take", "Dancing Queen", and a finale called "A Change Is Gonna Come". The titles quietly insist that music is a lineage of human work. Second, the show practised what it preached. Its score is by the composer Mocky, its contributors include Flying Lotus and Thundercat, and its leads' singing voices belong to the singers Nai Br.XX (Carole) and Celeina Ann (Tuesday). Mocky won Best Score at the Crunchyroll Anime Awards. A story about human music was made by a roster of named humans.

ANN's reviewer called the show's future "a logical endpoint" of how present-day society already values music. That is the right frame. The show is not science fiction about robots. It is a satire of an industry that already optimised for volume, polish and data, extended fifty years.

## A framework: what AI can copy, and what it can't

To test the show against today's evidence, I use a five-part model. A piece of music reaches a listener through five layers, and AI's reach is very different at each one.

- **Sound** is the audio itself: timbre, mix, polish, the feel of a genre.
- **Song** is the composition: melody, harmony, structure, lyrics.
- **Story** is the life the listener believes is behind the song: who made it, why, what it cost them.
- **Stage** is presence: a performance in a shared room, at a set time, that cannot be repeated exactly.
- **Stake** is accountability: someone who consented, gets paid, can be credited, and can be held responsible.

The model makes one prediction that the rest of this article tests. If AI makes sound and song abundant, their price falls towards zero, and value moves to the layers AI cannot supply. That means more weight on disclosure (so listeners can find the story), on live performance (the stage), and on consent and liability (the stake). It also predicts where the fights will be: wherever someone tries to borrow the scarce layers without paying for them, by faking a story, cloning a voice or training on a catalogue without permission.

Where does that volume come from? Mostly from tools that turn a text prompt into a finished song in seconds. Suno is the market leader. A Billboard report on its investor materials, cited by *Forbes Australia*, put its output at about 7 million songs a day. Its chief executive said in February 2026 that it had passed 2 million paid subscribers and $300 million in annual revenue, up from the $200 million it reported when it raised $250 million at a $2.45 billion valuation in November 2025. Seven months later it was worth more than twice that.

Here the show's prediction is both right and wrong. Right: AI really can make competent music at a scale no human industry could match. Wrong about the shape: Carole & Tuesday imagined AI music as a top-down studio product, Tao's lab manufacturing a handful of polished stars. The real thing is a consumer app used by tens of millions of people, producing an ocean of tracks that almost nobody hears.

This gap is the most important number in the debate. Fully AI-generated music is a large share of what is *uploaded* and a tiny share of what is *chosen*. And up to 85% of the streams those tracks did attract in 2025 were fraudulent, according to Deezer, which strips such streams out of royalty payments. Much of the flood is not an artistic movement. It is an attempt to skim the royalty pool with cheap content and fake plays.

That is why the economics matter even if listeners ignore AI tracks. Streaming royalties are paid from shared pools, so every fraudulent stream that is not caught dilutes the payment to everyone else. Deezer cites a study by the collecting-society body CISAC with PMP Strategy estimating that nearly a quarter of creators' revenues are at risk by 2028.

The platforms' first response has been hygiene rather than philosophy. Deezer removes detected AI tracks from algorithmic recommendations and editorial playlists, no longer stores hi-res versions of them, and says it detected more than 42 million AI-generated tracks and kept them out of recommendations between January 2025 and August 2026. In October 2026 it announced a setting, due before the end of the year, that lets listeners hide fully AI-generated tracks, albums and artists from search. Spotify said in September 2025 that it had removed more than 75 million spam tracks in the previous twelve months and announced a spam filter aimed at mass uploads, duplicates and artificially short tracks.

**What this means for the framework:** sound and song are no longer scarce, and the cost of producing them is close to zero. But abundance has not translated into attention. The scarce resource on Mars was never the hit; it was the listener who cared.

## Deep dive 2: story, and what changes when listeners know

If sound is copyable, the next question is whether listeners care where music comes from. The evidence says: not when they cannot tell, and quite a lot when they can.

Two findings sit side by side. Ninety-seven per cent of respondents failed the blind test: they heard two AI tracks and one human track and could not identify which was which. But 80% want fully AI-generated music clearly labelled, 52% think it should stay off the main charts, and 45% of streaming users would like to filter it out. People are curious about AI music (66% would try it once). They also want to know what they are listening to.

Laboratory studies explain why the label matters. In a 2023 paper, researchers at Missouri University of Science and Technology found that listeners liked music less when they believed an AI had composed it. The effect was conditional: when they told participants that electronic music was AI-made, liking did not change, which the authors put down to electronic music already sounding machine-like. With human-sounding classical excerpts, the AI label lowered liking. A 2025 study in *Empirical Studies of the Arts* went further. One hundred and twenty participants watched piano performances with identical audio, once attributed to a pianist and once to an AI-driven piano. They rated the "human" versions as more likeable, more engaging and of higher quality, and when asked what differed, they described differences in rhythm, tempo and dynamics that were not there. Belief about who made the music changed what people heard.

That is the story layer in action. A listener's experience of a song includes their model of the person behind it. Take the person away, or reveal that there never was one, and the same audio lands differently for many listeners. The show dramatised exactly this: Angela's songs are flawless, but the series keeps handing its emotional payoffs to the duo's rougher, self-written songs, which are plainly theirs.

The industry is now building disclosure into its plumbing:

- **Labels.** On 10 July 2026 an IFPI-led coalition including the RIAA, the Recording Academy, SAG-AFTRA and the Human Artistry Campaign launched voluntary track-level labels. "AI-Generated" means generative AI produced all or the primary part of the creative elements, such as the lead vocal. "AI-Assisted" means humans performed the lead vocal and primary instruments and AI supplied some elements.
- **Credits.** Spotify backed an industry standard for AI disclosures in song credits, and in April 2026 launched a beta letting artists say how they used AI. It is candid about the limit: "the absence of a credit doesn't mean AI wasn't used."
- **Identity.** In August 2026 Spotify began labelling "AI Persona" profiles, artist pages whose identity is AI-generated, and excluding their music from editorial and algorithmic recommendations by default unless a listener follows them. The label is about whether the artist is a real person, not about how the audio was made.
- **Charts.** From the chart dated 31 August 2026, Australia's ARIA charts exclude wholly AI-generated recordings; AI-assisted ones remain eligible if they are substantially human-made and raise no manipulation concerns. Ineligible tracks also cannot win ARIA Awards.
- **Storefronts.** Bandcamp, a marketplace where fans buy directly from artists, banned music generated wholly or in substantial part by AI in January 2026, along with using AI to imitate other artists.

Notice where every one of these lines is drawn: not at "any AI use", but at the human role. That is the framework's story and stake layers turned into policy.

## Deep dive 3: stage, and what two people in a room add

The emotional core of *Carole & Tuesday* is not a protest against machines. It is a partnership. Two people who could not be more different discover that their halves of a song fit together, and the show keeps returning to the moment when they play for someone: on a bridge, in an empty concert hall, in a bar for an audience of ten, on a festival stage in front of a hostile crowd.

It cannot stand in a room with you at a particular time, make a mistake, recover, and look at the person next to it. ## Deep dive 4: stake, meaning consent, credit and who answers for the work

The last layer is the one the show treated most lightly and the real world is fighting over hardest. Stake covers three questions: did the people whose work trained the model consent, who owns and is credited for the output, and who is liable when it goes wrong.

**Consent and training.** In June 2024 Universal, Sony and Warner sued Suno and Udio, alleging that both had trained on their recordings without permission. Two years on, the fronts look like this:

The settlements changed the products. Under its Warner deal, Suno said it would launch licensed models in 2026, restrict downloads to paying users, and let artists and songwriters opt in to the use of their name, image, likeness and voice. In September 2026 it launched v6, a model family it says was built with Warner, BMG and Believe. Universal and Sony responded on 18 September with a second suit in federal court in Massachusetts, after the judge in the original case refused in August to let them add more than 61,000 works to it. They argue that Suno's own licensing deals prove a market for training licences exists. Suno's product chief has said the revenue-share deals are not in exchange for training.

Europe is moving faster through the courts. In November 2025 the Munich Regional Court ruled for GEMA, the German music rights society, against OpenAI over the lyrics of nine songs, including Herbert Grönemeyer's "Männer". It found that the models had memorised the lyrics and could reproduce them, rejected OpenAI's reliance on the EU's text-and-data-mining exceptions, and held OpenAI rather than its users responsible. On 31 July 2026 the same court ruled for GEMA against Suno, finding it liable for soundalike outputs and for models stored on German servers, and rejecting a US fair-use defence for the training copies because the works resurfaced in the outputs. Both rulings can be appealed, and Suno has said it disagrees.

**Credit and ownership.** In the US, purely machine-made music cannot be owned. The US Copyright Office concluded in January 2025 that prompts alone do not give a user enough control to be an author, while AI-assisted works can still be protected for their human contributions. The courts agree: the D.C. Circuit upheld the human-authorship requirement in *Thaler v. Perlmutter* in March 2025, and the Supreme Court declined to hear the case on 2 March 2026. A fully generated song can top a chart, but in the US no one owns it.

**Voices and likeness.** Tennessee's ELVIS Act, signed on 21 March 2024 and in force from 1 July that year, added a person's voice, including simulations of it, to the likeness rights the state already protected. At federal level, the NO FAKES Act, which would create a right over digital replicas of a person's voice and likeness lasting up to 70 years after death, cleared the Senate Judiciary Committee unanimously on 18 June 2026 and awaits a full Senate vote. Spotify's policy since September 2025 allows vocal impersonation only when the impersonated artist has authorised it.

**Consent inside the industry.** The newest front is between artists and their own partners. On 22 June 2026 a coalition of artist, songwriter and manager organisations coordinated by the European Music Managers Alliance warned labels and publishers that creators were receiving letters saying their work had already been placed into AI licensing deals without discussion. They asked for three things: the right to refuse, a clear split of revenue, and information before any deal is signed.

<Note kind="insight">
The legal question has shifted from "is training on music allowed?" to "on whose terms?" Licences between AI firms and labels answer it for companies. They do not yet answer it for the people whose voices and songs are in the catalogue.
</Note>

## Case examples

**The Velvet Sundown: a story that did not survive disclosure.** In mid-2025 a "band" called The Velvet Sundown released about 40 tracks in roughly a month and passed a million monthly listeners on Spotify, helped by playlist placements. Amid speculation about who was behind it, a post from an account presenting itself as the project's official voice described it as a synthetic music project guided by human creative direction. The music had not changed; the story had, and the conversation moved from the songs to whether listeners had been misled. *The lesson:* AI can supply the sound and the song, but a fabricated story is a liability that grows with success.

**Xania Monet: AI sound, human stake.** Xania Monet is an AI-voiced R&B act created by Telisha "Nikki" Jones, a Mississippi songwriter who, according to *Billboard*, writes about 90% of the lyrics herself and uses Suno to produce the songs. Monet topped Billboard's R&B Digital Song Sales chart and reached No. 3 on Hot Gospel Songs, and Hallwood Media signed her to what Billboard described as a multimillion-dollar deal. Billboard estimated that five of her songs earned about $52,000 in just over two months. *The lesson:* the commercially durable AI acts so far have a real person with something at stake behind them. That is the framework's stake layer, supplied by a human.

**Breaking Rust: a song that charts without a singer.** Billboard counted at least six AI or AI-assisted acts that entered its charts in a few months of 2025, including the AI country act Breaking Rust, which appeared on the Emerging Artists and Country Digital Song Sales charts. *The lesson:* the song layer is genuinely copyable at a level that sells. But download charts are small markets, which is why chart compilers are now deciding what they are meant to measure.

**Bandcamp: a marketplace that sells the story.** Bandcamp's January 2026 policy bans music made wholly or substantially by AI and AI impersonation of other artists, and says it may remove music on suspicion of being AI-generated, so that fans can trust that what they buy was made by people. *The lesson:* for a direct-to-fan business, "made by a human" is not a moral stance. It is the product.

## Closer to home: two viral Filipino songs and the question of who made them

The Philippines produced 2026's clearest local test of this argument. Filipino listeners are loyal to local acts: in Spotify's 2025 Wrapped for the Philippines, eight of the ten most-streamed songs were local, led by Cup of Joe's "Multo". That loyalty is to people, so the country's AI-music arguments have been about whether there were people behind the songs at all.

There was a warning shot first. In October 2025 a rock cover of Cup of Joe's "Pahina" made with AI tools and released by an act called Renegade Stories briefly reached No. 1 on Spotify Philippines' Viral Songs chart. Band members reacted with open disbelief online, fans called it unfair to the original artists, the cover's makers said AI had been used only as a tool to enhance the recording, and the cover was later removed from Spotify.

**"Hawak Mo ang Beat": the accusation became the story.** "Hawak Mo ang Beat" ("You Hold the Beat") is a dance-pop track with budots drums that became a nationwide craze in early 2026. SPOT.ph described it playing everywhere from Facebook Reels and jeepney speakers to noontime TV and Zumba tutorials. DZRH reported on 20 March that it had passed 1 million Spotify streams, with Vice Ganda, Charo Santos and the SexBomb dancers among those posting dance versions and a teacher using it as a class energiser. By July, Manny V. Pangilinan had picked it to close an orchestral medley at First Pacific's 45th anniversary as the song to represent 2026.

Who made it was harder to pin down. SPOT.ph found that the earliest Spotify upload, in 2025, sat under an account called OPM Music Studio whose page did little to show who was behind it, and Spotify did not answer its questions. Online, people pointed to the cover art (gibberish text on a DJ booth, an artificial-looking crowd) as a sign of AI. Some outlets, DZRH among them, went further and described the track as AI-generated without citing any technical evidence. On GMA's *Kapuso Mo, Jessica Soho*, producer Gian Vergel said it sounded obviously AI to him.

The man who says he made it disagrees, firmly. Sylvain Hernandez, a French producer based in Leyte who performs as DJ Mogo and is married to a Filipina, told GMA that he made the track with his wife's help, that it grew out of Leyte cha-cha and the heavy drums of budots, that he built the beat with professional electronic software, and that no part of it was generated by AI. He later told SPOT.ph that he held the earliest verifiable upload, that the earlier date on another account was a metadata entry, and that he had project files and timestamps to show it. He first kept the singer's identity secret; in April the *Daily Tribune* reported that he had named a singer, Shane Reyes, on an online show, though without footage of the recording. As of 15 October 2026 nobody has published technical evidence (the tools used, project metadata or a voice analysis) that settles either the AI question or the upload question. This article treats it as what it is: a dispute, not a finding.

In the same news cycle, Pablo of SB19 called the use of artists' work without consent very unethical and said artists should control how their identity and work are used. FILSCAP, the Filipino songwriters' and publishers' society, urged people using generative AI tools to make sure the material they use is licensed.

**"Malunggay Pandesal": a meme with many versions and no clear author.** The second case is smaller and murkier. A novelty song about malunggay pandesal, the bread roll with moringa leaves in the dough, circulates on TikTok and YouTube in many versions. One YouTube upload dated 24 February 2026, with about 220,000 views by mid-October, calls itself the original and credits a lyricist. A Spotify release under the same title, dated 10 April 2026, is credited to a different act. I found no news report establishing who wrote the song first, and none of the listings say whether AI tools were used on any version. This article names no author because no reliable source does.

Where the show fell short as a forecast is just as instructive:

1. **It imagined AI as a studio, not an app.** On Mars, a few powerful producers run the machines. On Earth, more than 100 million people have used a single generator, and the problem is volume, not a handful of synthetic stars.
2. **It imagined polish, not spam.** The show's AI hits are good. The real flood is mostly low-effort content and fraud, and the damage is to the royalty pool rather than to anyone's ears.
3. **It skipped training.** Nobody on Mars asks what Tao's AI learned from. On Earth, that is the question in every courtroom from Boston to Munich.
4. **On Mars, everyone knew.** In the show, AI music is the open default. On Earth, 97% of listeners cannot tell, which is why the defining fight of 2026 is over labels rather than over taste.

## Counterpoints

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 1: AI music tools democratise creation">
Most people will never learn an instrument. Tools like Suno let millions make songs for the first time, for fun, for friends or for a business. Treating that as a threat is gatekeeping dressed up as principle.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
Agreed on the first half: the framework says plainly that sound and song are being democratised, and Suno's 2 million paying subscribers are evidence of real demand. But democratising *creation* is not the same as democratising *attention*. AI tracks are more than half of Deezer's peak-day uploads and 1–3% of its streams. The most successful AI act in this article, Xania Monet, works because a human songwriter supplies the words and the stake. The objection to these tools is not that amateurs use them. It is about whether the catalogues they learned from were licensed, and whether fraudulent uploads are allowed to dilute everyone's royalties.
</Note>

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 2: Human music has always used machines">
Synthesisers, drum machines, sampling and pitch correction were all called the death of real music. AI is the next tool. Drawing a line now is arbitrary.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
The industry and the law agree with this, which is why the line is not drawn at tools. The US Copyright Office protects AI-assisted works for their human contributions, the IFPI-led labels distinguish "AI-Assisted" from "AI-Generated", and ARIA keeps AI-assisted recordings eligible. The line sits at whether a human made the primary creative choices and performed the lead parts. A drum machine never asked to be the artist. A generator that writes, sings and plays the whole track, under a fictional persona, is a different thing.
</Note>

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 3: Listeners do not care">
Ninety-seven per cent of people cannot tell AI music from human music. If the audience cannot hear a difference, the source is irrelevant, and the market will settle it.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
Not being able to *hear* the source is not the same as not caring about it. The same survey found 80% want AI music labelled and 52% want it kept off the main charts. Experiments show identical music is liked less when it is attributed to an AI, at least for human-sounding styles, and that listeners even invent audible differences to justify the preference. The bias is conditional: one study found no effect for electronic music, and AI acts have found paying buyers. But a market can only settle what it can see, which is why disclosure is the precondition, not an afterthought.
</Note>

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 4: Licensing deals will settle all this">
Once every major label licenses an AI company, training is paid for, the lawsuits end, and the problem goes away.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
Licences settle disputes between companies. They do not, on their own, settle consent for the people in the catalogue. In June 2026 artist and songwriter organisations said creators were being told their work had already been placed in AI deals without discussion. And licensing has not ended litigation: Universal and Sony filed a fresh suit against Suno in September 2026, and German courts have ruled against both OpenAI and Suno. Deals are a necessary step. Consent, transparency and a clear revenue split for creators are the step after.
</Note>

### For artists

Your sound is no longer your moat on its own; your identity, audience and presence are. Say clearly how you use AI, so that "human-made" is a fact you can point to, and read any letter about AI deals before you opt into anything.

### For labels and platforms

The flood is a fraud and trust problem before it is a creative one. The platforms that keep listeners' trust will be those whose labels are accurate and whose recommendations do not quietly fill with synthetic personas. Licensing with labels is necessary; consent from creators is the next fault line.

### For listeners

You already vote with your attention. Use the labels and filters as they arrive, and if you value an artist's story and stage, spend where it reaches them: tickets, merchandise and direct purchases.

### For policymakers

A workable settlement is visible: human authorship for copyright, rights over digital replicas of voice and likeness, standardised disclosure, and enforcement against royalty fraud. Training consent remains open, with courts in different countries reaching different answers. In the Philippines, IPOPHL's draft guidelines already draw the human-contribution line for copyright registration; the disclosure and likeness bills pending in Congress are where labelling could become law.

### For builders

<Note kind="field">
I build production AI systems for a living, including an LLMOps evaluation platform that scores model outputs with an ensemble of evaluators and watches for drift. The lesson I would carry into music is that provenance, evaluation and disclosure are cheapest when they are designed in from the first release, not retrofitted after a lawsuit.
</Note>

Suno's own pivot to licensed data, artist opt-ins and download limits shows where this ends up. Build that way from the start: credit inputs, label outputs, and make it easy for an artist to say no.

## What to do next: a checklist

**If you make music**
1. Write down, for each release, what was human-made and what used AI, and submit it through your distributor's AI credit fields where they exist. Keep your project files and upload records: the "Hawak Mo ang Beat" dispute shows they may be your only proof.
2. Check what your label or publisher has agreed on AI, and ask whether your work is included in any training licence.
3. Protect the layers AI cannot copy: build a mailing list, play live, and make your story visible.

**If you run a label, distributor or platform**
1. Adopt the IFPI-led AI-Generated and AI-Assisted labels at track level.
2. Measure the share of your royalty pool going to tracks flagged as AI, and the share of those streams flagged as fraud.
3. Ask creators before their work enters an AI deal, and publish how the revenue is split.

**If you build AI music tools**
1. Use licensed training data, and be able to show it.
2. Watermark outputs and pass disclosure metadata downstream by default.
3. Block prompts that name living artists unless they have opted in.

**If you just listen**
1. Turn on AI labels and filters where your service offers them.
2. Go to one more gig this year than last year.

## Limitations and methodology

- **One platform's data.** The upload and stream figures come from Deezer, which is smaller than Spotify or Apple Music, relies on its own detection tool and sells that tool commercially. Other services may see different patterns; most do not publish comparable data.
- **Company figures.** Suno's subscriber, revenue and user numbers are the company's own claims or reports of its investor materials, not audited figures. Live Nation's figures are its own results.
- **Survey limits.** The Deezer/Ipsos survey was an online poll of adults aged 18–65 in eight countries, commissioned by a company with an interest in the result. A blind test with three tracks measures one kind of detection, not all of them.
- **Small experiments.** The listener-bias studies used modest samples (120 people in the performance study) and specific genres. One found no effect for electronic music. The bias is real but conditional.
- **Live music is not proof of cause.** Record attendance is consistent with value moving to the stage, but live growth predates AI music and is concentrated among large acts.
- **Law in motion.** Most of the cases cited are unresolved or open to appeal, settlement terms are confidential, and the NO FAKES Act has not passed. Everything here is as of 15 October 2026.
- **The show is fiction.** Plot details come from the official synopsis, Netflix's episode guide, ANN's review and published episode summaries. I have paraphrased scenes rather than quoting dialogue or lyrics, and I have left out plot points I could not verify.
- **The Philippine cases are disputes, not findings.** Reports on "Hawak Mo ang Beat" conflict on who uploaded it first, whether AI was used and who sang it, and no technical evidence has been published either way. Some outlets described it as AI-generated without citing evidence; I have not repeated that as fact. For "Malunggay Pandesal" I found no news coverage at all, so that account rests only on platform listings, and I name no author. The status of Philippine bills is as of a May 2026 review.
- **The framework is mine.** The five layers are a thinking tool, not a validated model.

## The song still needs someone to sing it

*Carole & Tuesday* imagined a world where machines could write a perfect hit, and then spent its whole run showing that a perfect hit was not the point. Seven years on, the machines can write the hit. Half the songs arriving at one streaming service on its busiest days are made by them, and almost nobody can hear the difference. What they still cannot do is stand on a stage, carry a story that is true, or put their name to a risk.

<Note kind="insight">
AI has made sound cheap. That makes the singer, the room and the signature more valuable, and it is why the real fights are over consent, credit and disclosure. The show saw it coming: on Mars, the miracle was never the song. It was who was singing it.
</Note>
