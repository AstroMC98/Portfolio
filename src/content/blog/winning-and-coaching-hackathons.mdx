<p class="lede">Every hackathon ends the same way. Dozens of exhausted teams, each convinced that what they built is the most impressive thing in the room, get a few minutes in front of people who have already seen twenty other projects that day. Many of those teams lose in the first thirty seconds, and not because their code is worse. They lose because the judge cannot yet tell who the product is for, whether it works, or why it matters.</p>

I have been on both sides of that table. I have finished on the podium at four hackathons, including first places in 2024 and 2025, and since late 2023 I have mentored and run hackathons as an instructor at CIIT and Eskwelabs. Seen from both sides, the lesson is the same: the teams that win are rarely the ones that built the most. A short, hyperfocused solution beats an extensive platform that ends up as one of many — and the advice I give the teams I mentor is the advice I follow myself.

My argument is this: **hackathons are not won by the team that builds the most, but by the team that makes a judge's three minutes easiest to score — and the same discipline that wins the room is what keeps a project alive afterwards.** That discipline has four parts, which I call the differentiation stack: a sharp user, real data, a demo that works on the one path that matters, and a credible next step. None of them requires heroic engineering. All of them require deciding, early, what not to build.

The article starts with the numbers — how long the odds are and how short the window is — then sets out a framework built from the published judging criteria of major organisers. Five deep dives follow: where teams land, what reliably loses, how to pitch, how to build and coach a team, and what happens after the prizes. It closes with case examples, objections, implications, a 36-hour playbook built on my own practice, and the limits of the evidence.

## Context: why this matters now

Hackathons are no longer a niche weekend hobby. Major League Hacking, the largest student hackathon league, reports that its 2025 season reached **more than 150,000 hackers across 184 hackathons and other programmes, producing over 12,500 projects**, and it planned more than 200 student hackathons for the 2026 season. NASA's International Space Apps Challenge drew **more than 114,000 participants** to 551 local events in 167 countries and territories in 2025, and judged more than 11,500 project submissions — for ten global awards.

The prizes are the visible part. The less visible part is that hackathons have become a hiring signal, a route into government innovation programmes, and — for many students and career-switchers — the first time anyone outside a classroom judges their work. That raises the stakes on understanding how judging actually works.

And judging is fast. MLH's organiser guide recommends science-fair-style judging and budgets **two minutes for presentation and demo, one minute for questions and scoring, and one minute for the judge to walk to the next table** — and tells judges they have about three minutes per team. ETHGlobal gives finalists four minutes to demo and three for questions. Google's Chrome Built-in AI Challenge 2025 on Devpost asked for a demo video of under three minutes. The UN's Big Data Hackathon and Datathon allowed up to ten minutes of video — the most generous window here, and still shorter than most team stand-ups.

## A framework: the scorecard, the stack and the sequel

Strip away the branding and every hackathon asks three questions in sequence. **Does the project clear the bar?** That is the scorecard — the published criteria. **Does it stand out from the other projects that also clear the bar?** That is the differentiation stack. **Does it have a life after Sunday?** That is the sequel. Most advice covers only the first. In my experience, winners are decided by the second: the teams that win keep asking what will make them stand out against the other entries. And most of the long-term value — for careers and for the problem itself — sits in the third.

### Layer 1: the scorecard

Published judging criteria look different on the surface but converge on five questions. MLH's standard rules weigh Technology, Design, Completion and Learning equally. ETHGlobal scores Technicality, Originality, Practicality, Usability and a "WOW factor". Google's Chrome Built-in AI Challenge 2025 used a pass/fail viability stage and then five equally weighted criteria — Functionality, Purpose, Content, User Experience and Technical Execution — with ties broken by the score for **Purpose**. The UN Datathon 2023 listed Theme, Innovation, Methods, Presentation and Visualization.

Two things stand out. First, no organiser gives points for the size of the build. "Completion" and "Practicality" ask whether the thing works, not how much of it there is. Second, where an organiser has to pick one criterion to break a tie, Google picked Purpose — whether the application solves an existing problem in a compelling way. That is a small rule with a large lesson.

### Layer 2: the differentiation stack

Clearing the scorecard gets a team into contention. Among the teams that clear it, four things separate the finalists. I think of them as a stack because each layer depends on the one beneath it.

**A sharp user** makes every other judgement easier. A judge who knows the product is for a hospital claims officer deciding which claims to audit can evaluate the design, the data and the impact in seconds. A judge told it is "for the healthcare sector" cannot.

**Real data** is the cheapest credibility in the room. It shows the problem exists, that the team understands its shape, and that the solution does something non-trivial. It also turns "Methods" and "Visualization" criteria from claims into evidence.

**A working demo path** is the layer most teams get wrong. Not a working product — a working *path*: one user, one task, start to finish, live. The rest of the product can be a clickable mock-up or a sentence. Judges score what they see work.

**A credible next step** answers the question every serious judge asks last: "And then what?" It is the bridge from Layer 2 to Layer 3.

### Layer 3: the sequel

The sequel is what happens after the event. The evidence (Deep dive 5) shows that most projects stop within a week, and that the factors that predict a short burst of post-event activity are different from the ones that predict survival. A team that has a sharp user and a credible next step has already done half the work of a sequel.

## Deep dive 1: where most teams actually land

Put the two most important layers on two axes — how sharp the user and problem are, and how much of the solution works live — and four archetypes appear. Most hackathon fields contain all four.

**The mechanism.** Problem sharpness and demo strength reinforce each other. A sharp user tells the team *which* path to make work, so the build is narrower and more likely to work live. A working path, in turn, makes the problem concrete for the judge. Teams that start from the technology end up bottom-right: they build broadly, nothing is quite finished, and the demo becomes a tour of features. Teams that start from the problem but never commit to a build end up top-left.

**How to place a team honestly.** Ask two questions — the first when the team stops debating and starts building, the second well before the deadline. Can every team member describe the user in one sentence, and give the same sentence? And can someone run the demo path, start to finish, right now — even with fake data? Two yeses put the team on course for the top-right. A no on the first is a scoping problem; a no on the second is a build-order problem.

**The failure mode.** A common trap is the slide into bottom-right during the night. A team starts with a sharp user, hits an interesting technical problem around midnight, and spends the next eight hours on it. By morning the technical work is impressive and the user has disappeared from the demo.

<Note kind="field" label="From the field: what the winning teams did differently">
The teams I have won with, and the ones I have seen win, did three things. They built, built and built — and they were not afraid to pivot, even hours before the deadline. They always scoped down to something achievable and went for the low-hanging fruit: solutions that could actually be implemented in the real world with the least effort or change. And they kept asking what would make them stand out against the other entries. Never settle for less, but don't overcompensate. You will sometimes lose to a flashier output. But if you want your solution to live beyond the event instead of being a one-time thing, simplicity never fails.
</Note>

## Deep dive 2: what doesn't work

The losing patterns are more consistent than the winning ones. Three of them come straight from my own experience as a competitor; the rest are spelled out in organisers' own rules and judges' public advice, and the research on pitching and on hackathon projects backs much of it up.

<Note kind="field" label="From the field: what didn't work">
Three things cost teams, including mine. First, building a novel solution designed to impress investors in a setting that is not a pitch competition. Second, building big: hackathons are time-bound, and an unfinished big solution is not a valid entry — so always plan around the timeline, and build small. Third, not knowing your judges. Be aware of their quirks and their specialties, because the same solution lands differently depending on who evaluates it.
</Note>

### Building an investor pitch in a build contest

A hackathon is not a pitch competition, and treating it like one is the first mistake I would warn any team against. Investor pitches reward vision, market size and novelty. Hackathon rubrics reward something else: MLH scores Completion, ETHGlobal scores Practicality — "could it be used by its target audience today?" — and Google scores Purpose. A novel idea built to impress investors tends to arrive as a bold story with very little that works. The vision belongs in the credible next step. The build should be the part that can be implemented now, with the least effort or change for the people who would use it.

### Building big

Hackathons are time-bound, and an unfinished big solution is not a valid entry. That single fact explains more losses than any other. Big plans fail in a predictable way: the team divides the work into many parallel features, none of which is finished when the clock runs out, and the demo becomes a tour of screens that almost work. The research suggests why this happens so often. Nolte, Chounta and Herbsleb's study of 592 Devpost projects found that teams' self-reported skills matched the technologies their projects needed poorly — a mean "skill matching" score of 0.25 on a 0-to-1 scale — which the authors read as teams being under-qualified for what they attempted, or choosing to learn new technologies on the job. Either way, it is the recipe for the half-finished demo. MLH scores "Completion — does the hack work?"; ETHGlobal scores "Practicality — how complete and functional is your project?" Neither asks how many features you started. Build small, and plan every hour around the timeline.

### Not knowing your judges

The same solution lands differently depending on who evaluates it. A panel of statisticians may probe the method; a sponsor's product lead may ask who would use it; a government panel may ask whether it fits existing processes. Each judge has quirks and specialties, and a team that knows them can put the right evidence first. The research on pitch evaluation (see Deep dive 3) shows how much evaluators' own lenses shape scores. Knowing your judges does not mean changing the solution for each of them. It means changing the emphasis: which question you answer first, and which piece of evidence goes on screen.

### The tech demo with no user

This is the most common pattern among technically strong teams, and the most frustrating to judge. The build is real. The architecture is clever. And the judge cannot score Purpose, Practicality or Usability because there is no one to be useful *to*. It is no accident that Google chose Purpose — "Are you solving an existing problem in a compelling manner?" — as its tie-breaker, or that one of the Devpost judges interviewed for its judging-tips article said she looks at whether teams "see the problem behind the solution." Technical excellence without a user tends to win the "best technical" side prize, if there is one, and lose the main event.

### Slideware

MLH's standard rules are unusually blunt: teams are "strongly encouraged to present a demo," and "pitches and presentations are discouraged." ETHGlobal advises no more than four bullet points per slide. Slides describe; demos prove. In a two-minute slot, every slide is time not spent proving.

### Ignoring the requirements

The cheapest way to lose is to be disqualified before anyone looks at the work. Google's Chrome AI Challenge used a pass/fail first stage that checked whether an entry "reasonably fits the theme and reasonably applies the required APIs." Every one of the five judges interviewed for Devpost's judging-tips article returned to the same point in different words: meet the basic guidelines, hit every requirement, give the required deliverables real time. It sounds obvious, and it is still one of the easiest ways to exit in the first round.

### Buzzword stacking

AI hackathons have created a new version of an old problem: the pitch that lists models, frameworks and acronyms instead of saying what the system does. The research on jargon is not kind to this habit. Shulman and colleagues, in a study of 650 adults, found that jargon disrupted people's ability to process information *even when definitions were provided*, and lowered their interest and sense of understanding. Judges are experts, and jargon is less costly with expert audiences — but hackathon panels are usually mixed, and the person who decides may be the sponsor's product lead rather than an engineer.

There is a nuance worth stating carefully. Using many technologies is not itself a losing move: Nolte and colleagues found that each additional technology was associated with 29% higher odds of a project being continued in the week after the event, and a later study by Imam and colleagues of 22,183 Devpost projects found that projects with more technologies were more likely to have their code reused. The problem is not the technology count. It is *naming* technologies as a substitute for showing what they do.

### No data story

Data-heavy hackathons — the UN events, Space Apps, most government challenges — reward a clear analytical narrative. The UN Datathon 2023 listed Methods and Visualization as separate criteria; NASA's 2025 global awards included "Best Use of Data" and "Best Use of Storytelling". A team that shows twelve charts without saying what any of them means has handed its data story to the judge, who has three minutes and will not write it for them.

<Note kind="field">
Data storytelling is a subject I have taught as well as practised — I gave a talk on "The Art of Data Storytelling and Data Visualization" at UST in March 2023. The rule that follows for hackathons is the simplest one: one chart, one sentence. If a team cannot say in a sentence what a chart shows and why the user should care, the chart is not ready for the demo.
</Note>

## Deep dive 3: the three-minute case

The pitch is where the differentiation stack becomes visible. The research on how experts evaluate pitches is not hackathon-specific, but it is consistent, and it points the same way as the organisers' rules.

**Preparedness beats performance.** In a study of business-plan presentations, Chen, Yao and Kotha had 55 investors rate 31 presentations at a university competition. Presenters who appeared *prepared* — who showed command of the substance — were more likely to win funding; visible excitement did not help. Pollack, Rutherford and Nagy found a similar pattern in televised pitches on *Shark Tank* and *Dragons' Den*: preparedness built what they call cognitive legitimacy, and that drove funding. In hackathon terms, the strongest signal a team can send is fluent command of its own user, data and demo path — not energy.

**Experts categorise fast.** Elsbach and Kramer's study of Hollywood pitch meetings, based on 36 interviews and observation of 28 pitch sessions, found that experienced decision-makers sorted pitchers into creative and uncreative "types" using early cues, and then judged the idea through that lens. The first 20 seconds of a hackathon pitch do the same work. A team that opens with a sharp user and a clear stake gets sorted as a contender. A team that opens with "So, in today's world…" gets sorted somewhere else.

**Judges are human, and judging is noisy.** Two findings are worth knowing, not because a team can control them, but because they argue for removing every other source of doubt. Bruine de Bruin found that scores in Eurovision and in figure-skating competitions rose with performance order — later performers tended to score higher regardless of the scoring procedure. And Brooks and colleagues found strong presenter effects in pitch evaluation.

### A three-minute structure that works

My first rule of pitching is to lead with a story: the why is the setup for the what. Showing your output before the judges understand the problem is always a mistake — they are looking at a solution with nothing to anchor it to. The structure below puts that rule into a three-minute slot. It maps to the scorecard in Exhibit 3 and fits the slots in Exhibit 2.

1. **0:00–0:30 — The why, as a short story.** "A claims officer at a provincial hospital reviews four hundred claims a week and can audit twenty. She picks by gut." A user, a pain, a stake. No history of the sector. No team introductions.
2. **0:30–1:40 — The demo path, live.** One user, one task, end to end, on real or realistic data. Narrate what the user sees and decides, not what the code does.
3. **1:40–2:20 — The evidence.** One chart or one number that shows the solution does something meaningful — and one honest limitation.
4. **2:20–3:00 — The next step.** Who would pilot it, what it needs, and what number would show it worked.

Then prepare for questions as seriously as for the pitch. ETHGlobal gives three minutes to Q&A against four for the demo; the questions are where judges test preparedness, and preparedness is what the research says they reward. The three questions every team should rehearse are: "Who exactly is this for?", "What happens when the data is wrong?" and "What would it take to run this for real?"

<Note kind="field" label="From the field: stitching the demo together">
On the demo side, before the age of vibe coding, iframes were a lifesaver for my teams. They let us stitch separate components together quickly for the demo, and let people build in parallel without everything having to live in one codebase. The principle outlasts the technique: make it cheap to assemble one working path from parts that different people build at the same time.
</Note>

## Deep dive 4: building and coaching the team

### Team size and composition

The evidence on team size is thinner than hackathon folklore suggests. In Nolte and colleagues' Devpost sample, teams averaged 3.3 members (range two to eight), and the hypothesis that smaller teams would be more likely to continue their projects found no support. Imam and colleagues found that projects with more participants were more likely to have their code reused. The UN Big Data Hackathon preferred teams of three to five and recommended mixing professional and academic backgrounds. Outside hackathons, Wu, Wang and Evans's analysis of more than 65 million papers, patents and software products found that small teams tend to disrupt while large teams tend to develop existing ideas — a reminder that a hackathon, which rewards a fresh angle, does not need a big team.

What the evidence does support is **skill diversity**. Nolte and colleagues found that teams with more diverse skills, and with skills that matched what their project needed, were substantially less likely to see the project die in the months after the event.

### My team: four people, four distinct jobs

My ideal hackathon team is four people with distinct jobs. Four sits comfortably inside the research and the rules — the UN's preferred three to five, and close to the Devpost average of 3.3 — but I should be clear that no study I found singles out four as the optimum, and Nolte and colleagues found team size made no measurable difference to whether projects continued. What the research does back is the principle underneath my split: distinct, complementary skills, matched to what the project needs.

Two features of this setup do most of the work. The first is the time split. I spend about half of a hackathon brainstorming — speaking out, discussing and arguing the idea down to its best version — rather than jumping straight into building. That sounds like a lot when the clock is running, and it is in tension with the build-first instinct most engineers bring. It works only because the second half is spent on something deliberately small: a scoped-down solution that four people can actually finish. The nearest research finding is indirect. Nolte and colleagues found that teams which did technical preparation before the event had about twice the odds of continuing their project in the following week — evidence that front-loaded thinking helps, though it measured preparation before the event, not brainstorming during it.

The second is the Talker. My next priority after the idea is always to talk to the mentors and coaches. They know the criteria, and they know things you don't. It is always additive: some advice will miss, but you lose nothing, because the Talker consults while the Builder builds. In a team of three, people double up; what matters is that every job has exactly one owner, and that the Main Builder's focus time is protected.

### What a coach actually does

Mentors are tempted to help by coding. It is usually the least valuable thing a coach can do. The advice I give the teams I mentor is the same advice I follow myself:

- **Spend the first half thinking, not building.** Argue the idea down to its best version before anyone opens an editor.
- **Talk to the mentors early.** They know the criteria, and things you don't; the Talker should be in those conversations from the start.
- **Scope down to something you can actually finish and implement.** An unfinished big solution is not a valid entry.
- **Don't be afraid to pivot late.** Even hours before the deadline, a sharper, smaller idea can beat a sunk cost.
- **When you pitch, lead with the why before you show the what.**

On top of those rules, a coach's leverage is in questions and timing. Before the build: "Who is the user, in one sentence?" and "What is the one path you will demo?" Once building starts: "Can you run that path end to end, right now?" Scope cuts are emotionally hard for a team and easy for an outsider. And before the pitch: run the full three minutes with a timer, twice.

<Note kind="field">
Since November 2023 I have been a data science instructor and mentor at CIIT and Eskwelabs, running workshops, bootcamps, hackathons and trainings on AI, no-code AI and data science for audiences that include government teams, executives and working professionals. The rules above are what I tell the hackathon teams I mentor there.
</Note>

### Preparation, within the rules

Preparation before the event has to respect the rules: MLH's standard rules say all work on a project should be done during the hackathon, and while a previously considered *idea* is allowed, reusing prior code or project materials is not. Legitimate preparation is everything that is not the project: researching the user, the domain and the judges, finding public datasets, setting up environments and agreeing roles. Check each event's rules; they differ.

## Deep dive 5: what happens after the hackathon

The prize ceremony is the end of most hackathon projects. The best evidence on this comes from Nolte, Chounta and Herbsleb, who linked 592 projects from 73 Devpost hackathons held between April and May 2018 to their GitHub repositories and tracked commits for five months.

More than half the teams (54%) said they intended to continue. Most did not. The more interesting finding is that **short-term and long-term continuation are different phenomena**, predicted by different things.

Three lessons follow.

**Winning gives a project a push, not a future.** A prize is associated with a burst of activity in the following days, and then the effect disappears. If the sequel matters, it has to be planned separately from the win.

**The post-hackathon sprint is a warning sign.** Projects with intense commit activity in the first week were *more* likely to be abandoned later. My reading — and it is an interpretation, not the paper's finding — is that a frantic week of polishing is often the team finishing the hackathon rather than starting the product. A short pause to decide who owns what, and for whom, may be healthier than a sprint.

**The sequel is about people, not code.** Long-term survival was associated with team skill diversity, skills matched to the project, and an intention to expand the project's reach — finding users, funding or partners. Those are exactly the things the differentiation stack asks for: a sharp user and a credible next step.

Code also lives on in less visible ways. Imam and colleagues traced code from 22,183 Devpost projects and found that only about 9% of the code in those projects was created during the hackathon itself — but roughly a third of that new code was later reused in other projects. Even when a project dies, its parts may not.

This is also where my bias for small, implementable solutions pays off. You will sometimes lose to a flashier output in the room. But a solution scoped to what can actually be implemented, with the least effort or change for the people who would use it, has a far better chance of living beyond the event than an extensive platform that ends up as one of many.

A hackathon entry that reaches a national research forum has moved from "a demo" to "a proposal someone can act on" — which is the whole point of a credible next step.
</Note>


## Case examples

**DOH-DAP Hack4Health 2024: a sharp user in a crowded field.** With more than 100 teams, a health hackathon attracts every flavour of "AI for healthcare." The winning entry was specific: a fraud-detection platform for unified health data. Fraud detection has a natural user (whoever decides which claims or records to investigate), a natural data story (which records look anomalous, and why) and a natural next step (a pilot on real records). The project was later presented as BANT.AI at a national DOH research forum. *The lesson:* in a large field, specificity is a differentiator in itself.

**UN Big Data Hackathon, youth track: a data story for a global panel.** My team placed 2nd and won Best Youth Team, Asia, out of more than 120 teams. The UN's hackathon runs on data and on video: its published rules ask teams for a presentation, a voice-over video of no more than ten minutes and their code, and youth teams compete from around the world. The UN's published criteria for its 2023 Datathon put Methods and Visualization alongside Theme, Innovation and Presentation. *The lesson:* when the judges are statisticians, the data story *is* the demo.

**ICSC Hack-A-Street 2025: a simple model for a concrete question.** The winning entry among 13 teams used a gravity model for retail site selection — a classic, interpretable approach that answers a concrete business question: where should the next store go? *The lesson:* a model a judge can understand in one sentence is easier to score than a more complex one they have to take on trust, and easier to implement afterwards.

**DICT AI.DEAS for Impact 2025, Bohol: a vulnerability score for a named place.** In a case I was not involved in, a UP Cebu computer-science team won a DICT regional AI hackathon with Aegis, a health data platform that combined health information into a single dengue vulnerability score for Cebu City barangays, explained the factors behind each score and suggested interventions for individuals and hospitals. *The lesson:* a sharp user (barangay and hospital decision-makers), real data (dengue risk factors) and a clear output (a score with reasons) is a winning pattern well beyond my own teams.


## Counterpoints

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 1: Judges reward technical difficulty — scoping down looks weak">
Hackathons are engineering contests. MLH scores Technology and ETHGlobal scores Technicality. A team that ships one narrow path will look less impressive than a team that attempts something ambitious, and judges know the difference.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
Technical difficulty is scored — but it is one criterion among four or five, and it is scored on what *works*. MLH pairs Technology with Completion; ETHGlobal pairs Technicality with Practicality; Google breaks ties on Purpose. A narrow path can be technically hard: the difficulty goes into making one thing work on real data, including the awkward case, rather than into ten things that half-work. Ambition that does not reach the demo scores zero on every criterion. I will be honest about the cost: you will sometimes lose to a flashier output. But a simple solution that works is rarely the reason a team loses, and it is the one most likely to live beyond the event.
</Note>

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 2: Judging is a lottery, so strategy barely matters">
Order effects, presenter bias and tired judges mean outcomes are noisy. With ten global awards for 11,500 submissions, winning is mostly luck.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
The noise is real — Exhibit 8 and the order-effect research make that plain — and nobody should treat a single result as a verdict on their ability. But noise argues *for* the stack, not against it. When impressions drift, unambiguous evidence on screen is the best anchor a team has. And most hackathons are not Space Apps: in a field of 13 or 100 teams, the losing patterns in Exhibit 7 eliminate a large share of the competition before luck comes into it.
</Note>

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 3: Hackathons are for learning, not winning">
MLH literally scores "Learning". Optimising for the judges turns a learning event into a pitching contest and pushes beginners away from trying new things.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
Partly right, and I would not coach a first-time team to optimise only for the prize. But the stack *is* a learning curriculum: scoping to a user, working with real data, shipping something that works end to end and explaining it in three minutes are the skills most junior engineers lack, and the ones employers ask about. Learning a new framework is valuable; learning to cut scope under a deadline is more valuable, and harder to learn anywhere else.
</Note>

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 4: Most projects die anyway, so the sequel is irrelevant">
If fewer than 4 in 100 projects are active after five months, planning a next step is theatre for the judges.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
The low survival rate is the reason to plan the sequel deliberately, not to ignore it. Nolte and colleagues found that the teams whose projects lasted were the ones with diverse, well-matched skills and a stated intention to reach users or funding — things a team can decide on. And the value of a credible next step is not only survival: it is what lets a project be picked up by an agency, a sponsor or a forum, as the Hack4Health entry was.
</Note>

### For participants

Treat the published criteria as the specification and the differentiation stack as the design. Spend the first half thinking; decide the user and the demo path before you open an editor, and talk to the mentors early. Get real data early. Record a backup video of the demo path before the final hour. And if you win, decide who owns the next step before the celebrations end.

### For coaches and mentors

Your leverage is in questions, timing and permission to cut. Use the first half to push the team through the four stack questions; once building starts, check that the demo path runs end to end; time the pitch twice. Tell the team it is never too late to pivot to something smaller and sharper. Resist the urge to fix the build yourself — a team that wins on a mentor's code has not learned anything it can repeat.

### For organisers and sponsors

If you want projects you can adopt, your criteria should say so. Google's choice of Purpose as a tie-breaker is a good model; so is MLH's preference for demos over pitches. And if continuation matters to you, the research suggests prizes are not enough: support for winners in the months after the event — access to data, a named contact, a pilot — is what turns a burst into a sequel.

### For hiring managers

A hackathon trophy is weak evidence on its own; judging is noisy and team credit is shared. A candidate's account of a hackathon is strong evidence. Ask what they cut and why, what data they used, what broke in the demo and what happened afterwards. The answers reveal scoping judgement and composure under pressure — the same things that matter in client-facing engineering.

**The pre-flight checklist**, to be answered in writing before the team starts building:

1. Who is the user? (One role. One sentence.)
2. What decision or task do they struggle with today?
3. What data will we show, and where does it come from?
4. What is the one demo path, start to finish?

If a team cannot answer questions 1, 4 and 5, it is not ready to write code.

## Limitations and methodology

- **The continuation evidence is old and narrow.** Nolte and colleagues studied 592 projects from 73 Devpost hackathons over one month in 2018, mostly collegiate and all collocated. Continuation was measured as commits to a linked GitHub repository, which misses projects that continued elsewhere. Online and AI-era hackathons may behave differently.
- **Associations are not causes.** Winning, preparation and skill diversity are associated with continuation; the study does not show that they cause it. Teams that win may differ in many unmeasured ways.
- **Judging criteria vary by event.** The four organisers in Exhibit 3 are a convenience sample of well-documented events, not a representative survey. The UN criteria were labelled tentative, and ETHGlobal does not publish weights.
- **The pitch research is mostly not about hackathons.** The preparedness, categorisation, order-effect and bias studies come from investor pitches, film pitches and arts competitions. I use them as analogues; their effect sizes should not be assumed to transfer.
- **The Philippine examples are illustrative.** Public documentation of Philippine government hackathons is patchy; I have cited only what organisers or universities published, and could not find published judging criteria for those events.
- **My frameworks and practice are mine.** The scorecard mapping, the differentiation stack, the archetypes, the rubric, the four-role team, the roughly 50% thinking split and the playbook come from competing in and coaching hackathons. They are tools for thinking, not validated instruments, and my own results are a small, self-selected sample. Where my practice meets the research — team size, front-loaded thinking — the research is consistent with it but does not test it directly.

## Build less, prove more

Hackathons reward a particular kind of courage: the willingness to build one thing that works instead of ten things that might. The published criteria, the time limits and the research on how experts judge pitches all point the same way. Judges score what they can see working, for someone they can picture, in the few minutes they have. And the projects that outlive the weekend are the ones whose teams knew, before the build, who they were building for and what would come next.

<Note kind="insight">
Nobody wins a hackathon for what they almost built. Pick one user, show real data, make one path work, and say what happens next — in that order, in under three minutes.
</Note>
