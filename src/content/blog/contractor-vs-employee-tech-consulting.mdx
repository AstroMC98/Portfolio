<p class="lede">The question "should I go freelance?" usually arrives with a number attached: a day rate a friend charges a foreign client, multiplied by twenty working days, compared with a monthly salary. On that arithmetic, independence always wins. It is also the wrong arithmetic. A salary is not just a price for your time. It is a bundle — pay, benefits the law requires, insurance against bad months, and someone whose job is to make you better — and when you leave, you have to rebuild every part of that bundle yourself.</p>

My argument is this: **independent contracting in tech consulting is a trade, not a raise. Under Philippine rules the tax often favours the contractor, so the real price of independence is unbilled days and uninsured risk — and it pays only for people with the pipeline and runway to carry them.** Get those two right — and accept that selling yourself and defending scope are now part of the job — and contracting can be the better deal on money, autonomy and growth. Get them wrong and the higher rate is an illusion that lasts until the first gap between clients.

<Note kind="field">
I have worked on both sides of this line. From 2022 to 2024 I was an employee — a Data Scientist at AIA Digital+, then Global Data Science Manager at TDCX AI in Singapore. Since September 2024 I have worked as an independent contractor: Lead AI Engineer and Tech Consultant for Intelligent AI Solutions, a UK enterprise AI consultancy, delivering remotely from the Philippines. In March 2025 I gave a talk at AIM Career Services on breaking into tech consulting within five years. This article is the longer, more rigorous version of that subject.
</Note>

<Note kind="field" label="How I landed my contract">

## Context: why this matters now

Four things have changed around independent technical work, and all four can be measured.

**The market for independent skilled work is large and growing.** The World Bank's 2023 report *Working Without Borders* estimates between 154 million and 435 million online gig workers worldwide — up to 12% of the global labour force — and finds that demand in developing countries is growing faster than in developed ones. In the United States, Upwork's 2023 *Freelance Forward* survey estimated that 64 million Americans freelanced that year, 38% of the workforce, earning about $1.27 trillion.

**Skilled professionals in particular are moving.** Upwork's *Future Workforce Index 2026*, published on 14 July 2026 and based on a survey of 2,400 US skilled workers in March–April 2026, found that skilled freelancers now make up 38% of US knowledge workers, up from 28% a year earlier — and that 58% of full-time employees say they are considering freelancing, up from 36%. It also reported that freelancers doing AI work on its marketplace earn 34% more per hour than those who do not.

**The Philippines is already plugged into this market.** A 2022 study by the Department of Information and Communications Technology (DICT) with Payoneer found that 42.9% of Filipino freelancers surveyed had clients outside the country, and the companion Payoneer–GCash *Philippines Freelance Market 2022* report found that freelancers serving both local and foreign clients earn as much as 57% more per hour than those serving only local ones. Coverage of that report cited around 1.5 million Filipinos signed up to international freelancing platforms, a figure attributed to the Creative Economy Council of the Philippines. But the mix is not yet weighted towards technical work: a 2021 Philippine Institute for Development Studies (PIDS) study, drawing on the Online Labour Index, found that only about 14% of Filipino online workers were in software development and technology roles.

That last number is the opportunity for technical consultants. Filipino independent workers are already selling to the world; relatively few are selling the high-value technical work — AI engineering, data platforms, systems integration — that commands the strongest rates. The question is not whether that market exists. It is whether a given person, at a given point in their career, should leave employment to serve it.

The fourth change is less visible but matters most for this article: **the rules have shifted under people's feet.** Social security contributions reached 15% in January 2025, the last step of a schedule set in 2019. The Ease of Paying Taxes Act changed how the self-employed register, invoice and file from January 2024. The temporary 1% percentage tax ended in mid-2023. Much of the advice circulating among freelancers predates these changes.

## The legal line: what makes you a contractor in the Philippines

Before money, status. A Filipino tech worker can be an employee on a payroll, an independent contractor with their own Bureau of Internal Revenue (BIR) registration, or — the uncomfortable middle — someone called a contractor who is, in law, an employee. The label on the contract does not settle the question. As the Supreme Court put it in *Royale Homes Marketing Corp. v. Alcantara* (2014), the existence of an employer–employee relationship "is a matter of law."

### The four-fold test

Philippine courts apply a **four-fold test**: (1) who selected and engaged the worker; (2) who pays the wages; (3) who holds the power of dismissal; and (4) who has the power to control the worker "with respect to the means and methods by which the work is to be accomplished." The fourth element is the decisive one; the Court in *Royale Homes* calls it "the most determinative factor."

The distinction inside the control test is precise and useful. Rules that "merely serve as guidelines towards the achievement of the mutually desired result" do not make someone an employee. Rules that dictate the means or methods do. A client who says *"the retrieval system must answer within two seconds and respect our access controls"* is specifying a result. A client who says *"log in at 9, attend our stand-up, use our laptop, and follow our sprint process"* is getting close to controlling the method.

### The economic-reality layer

Where control alone does not settle it, the Court has used a **two-tiered test**. In *Francisco v. NLRC* (2006) it combined the control test with "the underlying economic realities of the activity or relationship," and listed factors including how integral the work is to the business, the worker's investment in equipment, the worker's opportunity for profit and loss, the initiative and skill required, the permanence of the relationship, and "the degree of dependency of the worker upon the employer."

The two leading cases point in opposite directions, which is what makes them useful. In *Royale Homes*, the Court held that a real estate sales executive was an independent contractor: company rules and evaluations concerned results rather than methods, he kept no fixed hours, he was paid in commissions with no payroll or benefit registrations, and he could pursue other business. In *Francisco*, the Court found an employment relationship even though there was no written agreement, pointing to direct supervision by the company's technical consultant and to economic realities such as six years of service, salary and 13th-month pay records, and registration with the Social Security System.

**Why this matters to a tech contractor.** Misclassification cuts both ways. A Filipino company that engages you as a "contractor" while controlling you like staff may owe you 13th-month pay, social security contributions and separation pay — and you may be giving those up without knowing it. Conversely, if you want the freedoms of contracting, you have to behave like a business: own your tools, agree outcomes rather than hours, invoice, and keep other clients possible.

For international clients the picture is different again. Other countries run their own tests, and they change. The United Kingdom's off-payroll working rules (IR35) were reformed from 6 April 2021 so that, in most cases, medium and large clients decide a worker's status; GOV.UK last updated its guidance in February 2026. In the United States, the Department of Labor proposed in February 2026 to rescind its 2024 independent-contractor rule and return to an analysis centred on control and the opportunity for profit or loss. Those rules mainly govern people working within those countries' tax and labour systems, and how they touch a Philippine-based contractor depends on the facts — which is why a written contract that describes outcomes, deliverables and independence is worth more than any template you find online.

<Note kind="warn">
There is still no national freelancer protection law in the Philippines. The House approved House Bill 6718, the Freelance Workers Protection Act, by 250 votes in February 2023 — it would have required written contracts and penalised payment more than 15 days late — but it did not become law in that Congress. Until a law exists, your contract is your protection.
</Note>

If you are employed in the Philippine private sector, the law guarantees you a set of benefits that do not appear in your monthly salary figure. When you become a contractor, every one of them disappears or becomes your own expense.

A few of these deserve a closer look.

**13th-month pay** comes from Presidential Decree 851 (1975): one-twelfth of the basic salary earned in the calendar year, payable by 24 December, for rank-and-file employees who have worked at least a month. Under the TRAIN law, 13th-month pay and similar benefits are tax-free up to ₱90,000 a year. For a contractor it is simply a month of income that no one pays unless you priced it in.

**Social insurance** is where the employer subsidy is largest. Under the Social Security Act of 2018 (RA 11199), the SSS rate reached 15% of the monthly salary credit in January 2025, split 10% employer and 5% employee, with the salary credit running from ₱5,000 to ₱35,000. The same law says a self-employed member "shall pay both the employer and the employee contributions." PhilHealth's rate is 5% of monthly basic salary, with a floor of ₱10,000 and a ceiling of ₱100,000, as its January 2025 advisory confirmed; for employees the premium is shared equally. Pag-IBIG's maximum fund salary rose to ₱10,000 from February 2024, with 2% from each side, and self-employed members are treated as both employee and employer.

**Leave and separation** are the benefits people forget. Article 95 of the Labor Code gives every employee with at least a year of service five days of paid service incentive leave; most tech employers give more by policy. Articles 283 and 284 (now renumbered 298 and 299) require separation pay when employment ends for authorised causes — one month per year of service for redundancy or labour-saving devices, half a month per year for retrenchment, closure not due to serious losses, or disease, and never less than one month. Resignation, it should be said, does not trigger statutory separation pay.

Employers also tend to add things the law does not require: health maintenance organisation (HMO) cover, equipment, paid training, bonuses. None of those are counted in the calculation later in this article, which makes it conservative.

What I miss from employment is having a team around me by default, and benefits I never had to think about. What I would not give up is choosing which projects I pursue, and having a seat at the table where decisions are actually made, rather than being handed the outcome.
</Note>

## Deep dive 2: tax — where the contractor quietly wins

Here is the counter-intuitive part. For many Filipino tech contractors earning under ₱3 million a year, **tax is lower than for an employee on the same money**, because of an option introduced by the TRAIN law (RA 10963) in 2018.

**Two regimes.** A self-employed individual or professional in the Philippines is taxed by default on the same graduated schedule as employees — since 1 January 2023, 0% up to ₱250,000 of taxable income, then rising in bands of 15%, 20%, 25% and 30% to 35% above ₱8 million. Under the graduated route you deduct either itemised expenses or an optional standard deduction of up to 40% of gross business or professional income, and, if you are not VAT-registered, you also pay a 3% percentage tax on gross receipts. That 3% was cut to 1% from July 2020 to June 2023 under the CREATE Act and returned to 3% on 1 July 2023.

**The 8% option.** If your gross sales or receipts and other non-operating income do not exceed the ₱3 million VAT threshold, and you are not VAT-registered, you may instead elect to pay **8% on gross receipts above ₱250,000**, "in lieu of the graduated income tax rates and percentage tax." You elect on your first quarterly return of the year; miss it and you are on the graduated rates for the whole year. The ₱250,000 allowance applies only to the purely self-employed: if you also earn a salary — a mixed-income earner — the 8% applies to all your business receipts, with no ₱250,000 deduction.

**The paperwork is real.** Registering as a self-employed professional means a BIR registration, invoices for every engagement and quarterly filings: quarterly income tax returns (BIR Form 1701Q) and an annual return, plus quarterly percentage tax returns (Form 2551Q) if you are on graduated rates. The Ease of Paying Taxes (EOPT) Act, signed on 5 January 2024, made some of this easier: it removed the ₱500 annual registration fee from 22 January 2024, replaced the official receipt with the sales invoice as the document for services, allowed filing and payment at any revenue district office or authorised channel, and created lighter rules for micro and small taxpayers.

**The ceiling matters.** The 8% option ends at ₱3 million. Above it you must register for VAT and the graduated rates apply, so the arithmetic changes — and international service income has its own VAT questions that are worth settling with an accountant before you cross the line, not after.

<Note kind="insight">
The tax gap between employment and contracting is often the reverse of what people fear. For a Filipino contractor under ₱3 million, tax is usually the smallest problem. Utilisation — how many days you actually bill — is the biggest.
</Note>

<Note kind="field">
I registered as self-employed and use the 8% option. The setup is harder than people expect. My advice is to hire an accountant once you are settled in — it pays for itself.
</Note>

## Deep dive 4: the break-even — how much more a contractor must bill

Now the central calculation. The question is not "what day rate sounds good?" but "what would I need to bill to be no worse off than as an employee?" The numbers below are **illustrative assumptions, not my real figures or anyone else's.** Change them for your own case.

**The employee.** A senior tech professional earning ₱150,000 a month in basic salary, plus 13th-month pay: ₱1,950,000 in cash a year. Their share of SSS, PhilHealth and Pag-IBIG at the ceilings is ₱53,400 a year; their employer pays another ₱74,400 on their behalf. After the ₱90,000 13th-month exclusion and mandatory contributions, taxable income is ₱1,806,600 and income tax is ₱354,150. **Take-home pay: ₱1,542,450**, with full social insurance and paid holidays and leave.

**The contractor.** To match that, a contractor needs the same take-home *and* the same social insurance, which they now pay in full — ₱127,800 a year at the ceilings. Add business costs the employer used to carry (equipment, software, connectivity, an accountant), assumed here at ₱120,000 a year, and 2% of receipts for payment friction. On the 8% option, the break-even is **₱1,966,944 of gross receipts a year** — barely above the employee's cash pay, because the 8% regime takes only about ₱137,000 in tax.

- **Every 20 lost billable days costs about 10% of income.** A contractor who expected 220 days and gets 180 — because a project ends early and the next takes six weeks to land — needs a rate 22% higher to end the year in the same place.
- **The ₱3 million ceiling changes the shape of the curve.** Above it, the 8% option disappears and the graduated rates and VAT apply. On the graduated route with the 40% standard deduction, the same break-even rises to about ₱2.12 million in receipts. Higher earners need to redo this calculation, not extrapolate it.

It also ignores the contractor's upside: international clients who pay well above Philippine salary benchmarks, the 57% hourly premium the Payoneer–GCash study found for freelancers with foreign clients, and the freedom to take a second client.

<Note kind="field" label="My early mistake: scope creep">
Early on, I treated scope creep as normal and never contested it. Every "small additional request" got absorbed. The result was longer timelines, missed opportunities because I was stuck on stretched engagements, and eventually burnout. The fix was to treat scope as something you agree on, defend and renegotiate out loud. Saying "that's outside what we agreed — here's what it would take" is professional, not difficult.
</Note>

### The risks, named honestly

- **Income volatility.** The most common problem freelancers report is not rates but demand: in Payoneer's 2023 survey of more than 2,000 freelancers in 122 countries, 73% named finding new clients as their biggest challenge.
- **Selling never stops.** An employee sells themselves at interviews; a consultant does it at the start of every engagement. Time spent winning work is unbilled time, which is why the utilisation assumptions in Exhibit 8 matter so much.
- **Scope creep.** Every absorbed "small request" is unpaid time, especially on fixed-price and retainer work, and stretched engagements crowd out the next opportunity.
- **Client concentration.** One client paying all your bills is employment without the protection. If that client ends the contract, there is no separation pay and often little notice.
- **No benefits by default.** Exhibits 3 and 4 are now your problem: contributions, leave, health cover and retirement savings all need a line in your budget.
- **Misclassification.** If a client controls you like staff, you may be giving up rights you are owed; if you behave like staff, you may lose the tax and legal footing of a business.
- **Weak legal backstop.** With no national freelancer protection law, late payment is a contract dispute, not a labour standards violation.
- **Isolation and stalled growth.** Nobody is assigned to review your work or plan your development. For early-career engineers, this is the most expensive risk on the list.

### The upside, also named honestly

- **Rate.** International clients and specialised skills pay more. The 57% premium for Filipino freelancers with foreign clients and Upwork's 34% premium for AI work both point the same way.
- **Tax efficiency under ₱3 million**, as Exhibit 5 shows.
- **Autonomy** over which problems you take, how you work and when.
- **Variety and accelerated learning.** Several clients in a year can expose you to more architectures, industries and failure modes than several years inside one company.
- **International exposure.** Working directly with clients abroad builds a reputation that travels further than a local job title.

<Note kind="field">
My own independent years have been a portfolio rather than a single contract. Alongside the UK engagement, I have taught as a Data Science Instructor and Mentor with CIIT and Eskwelabs since November 2023 — workshops, bootcamps, hackathons and trainings for government, executives and professionals — and from September 2024 to December 2025 I was Senior Data Science Consultant to the Knowledge Innovation Division of Philippine Science High School. Concurrent roles like these are normal for independent consultants; they are worth explaining on a CV rather than hiding.
</Note>

<Note kind="field" label="What changed most">
In corporate, the only time I really had to sell myself was in interviews. In consulting, selling myself is my bread and butter: every engagement starts with convincing a client that I am the right person for their problem. Pay is tied to engagements rather than a fixed salary — less predictable, with a higher ceiling — and stability is something I build myself instead of something an employer provides. Learning changed too. Instead of going deep in one domain, I have been pulled across insurance, media, real estate and government, and every client system teaches me something about what breaks in production.
</Note>

## Case examples

**Royale Homes v. Alcantara (2014): how to be a contractor in law.** A real estate sales executive — reappointed on one-year terms since 1994, latterly as a division vice-president for sales — filed a complaint for illegal dismissal in 2003, claiming he was an employee. The Supreme Court disagreed. The company's rules, codes and evaluations concerned results, not methods; he kept no fixed hours; he was paid in commissions, with no payroll or social security registrations; and he could pursue other business. Even an exclusivity clause and repeated contract renewals did not, on their own, make him an employee. *The lesson:* the cleanest independent arrangements specify outcomes, leave methods to you and allow other clients. If your "contract" reads like a job description, expect it to be treated like one.

**Francisco v. NLRC (2006): when the label fails.** A worker who had served for six years under titles including accountant, liaison officer, acting manager and corporate secretary, with no written agreement, was found to be an employee. The Court introduced the two-tiered test because control alone could not capture the relationship, and the economic realities — years of service, payroll-style records, SSS registration and dependence on the company — together with direct supervision pointed to employment. *The lesson for both sides:* a company that engages Filipino tech talent as "consultants" while integrating them as staff is accumulating liabilities for 13th-month pay, contributions and separation pay. A worker in that position should know what they may be owed.

**The Freelance Workers Protection Act (2023): protection that did not arrive.** In February 2023 the House approved House Bill 6718 with 250 votes. It would have required written contracts signed by both sides, penalised hiring without a contract or paying more than 15 days late with fines of ₱50,000 to ₱500,000, let freelancers bring complaints to the Department of Labor and Employment for mediation, and directed the BIR to set up an assistance desk for freelancers. It did not become law in that Congress. *The lesson:* independent workers in the Philippines are protected by what they negotiate. A written contract with payment terms, late-payment consequences and an exit clause is not bureaucracy; it is your only safety net.

**The 2025–2026 cost reset: why old advice is stale.** In two years, SSS contributions reached 15%, the maximum salary credit rose to ₱35,000, Pag-IBIG's fund salary doubled to ₱10,000, PhilHealth's rate settled at 5% with a ₱100,000 ceiling, the 1% percentage tax expired, and the EOPT Act rewrote registration and invoicing. *The lesson:* a break-even calculation built on a blog post from 2021 will understate the contractor's costs. Redo the numbers every year.

## Counterpoints

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 1: The numbers clearly favour contracting">
Tax is lower, international rates are higher, and surveys show freelancers earning premiums. If a Filipino engineer can bill a foreign client at international rates, the comparison with a local salary is not close.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
Partly right, and this article concedes more than most: under ₱3 million, the 8% regime does favour the contractor (Exhibit 5). But the comparison people make is rate times twenty days against salary, and that ignores utilisation, which is where independence is won or lost (Exhibit 8). The surveys behind the premiums are also vendor surveys of people who are currently freelancing — the ones who stopped because they could not find clients are not in the sample. Contracting can pay much more. It pays more reliably for people with a pipeline.
</Note>

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 2: Employment is no safer — companies lay people off all the time">
Tech layoffs have shown that a permanent contract is not permanent. An employee with one employer has total client concentration; a contractor with three clients is more diversified.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
This is a fair point, and it is why the matrix in Exhibit 11 has a top-right quadrant at all. A contractor with two or three clients and a reserve can be more resilient than an employee at a fragile company. But the employee who is let go for redundancy is owed at least a month's pay per year of service by law; the contractor whose client leaves is owed whatever the contract says, and often that is nothing. Diversification is a real advantage — but only once you have achieved it. The first year of contracting usually looks like one client, which is the least diversified position there is.
</Note>

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 3: You do not need a company to grow — the internet is your mentor">
Courses, open source, communities and AI tools mean that structured corporate mentorship matters less than it used to. Contractors learn faster because they see more problems.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
For experienced engineers, largely true — variety is one of contracting's real advantages. For early-career engineers, I disagree. What a good team gives you is not information but feedback: someone who reviews your design before it ships and tells you why it will break. Clients rarely do that; they pay for results and leave when they do not get them. That is why "need for structured mentorship" is one of the five factors in the scorecard, and why I would rarely advise someone with under two years of shipped work to go fully independent.
</Note>

<Note kind="counter" label="Objection 4: Contractor status is a legal grey zone — why take the risk?">
Between misclassification rules at home and status rules abroad, contracting exposes both sides to legal uncertainty. Employment is simpler.
</Note>

<Note kind="response">
Simpler, yes; but the uncertainty is manageable when the work is genuinely independent. The Supreme Court's own test gives a clear line — results versus means and methods — and *Royale Homes* shows contractor arrangements surviving scrutiny. The risk is concentrated in arrangements that are employment in all but name: one client, fixed hours, the client's tools and process, indefinitely. If that describes your contract, the answer is not to avoid contracting but to fix the arrangement, or to ask for the job.
</Note>

## Implications by stakeholder

### For early-career engineers (under about three years)

Stay employed, and use the time deliberately. The things that make independence work later — shipped systems you can show, a reputation with people who will refer you, and a reserve of savings — are easiest to build inside a good team. Start testing demand only where your employment contract allows: many contracts restrict outside work and assign intellectual property, so read yours before you take a side project.

### For mid-career and senior engineers

You are the group for whom contracting most often pays. Run the scorecard, build the break-even calculation with your own numbers, and treat the first year as running a business: register properly, elect your tax regime deliberately, set aside contributions and tax every month, and work to add a second client before the first one ends.

### For hiring managers and clients engaging Philippine contractors

Engage contractors on outcomes, not hours. If you need someone to attend your stand-ups, use your laptop and follow your process indefinitely, you probably need an employee or an employer-of-record arrangement — and treating that person as a contractor can create liabilities for 13th-month pay, contributions and separation pay in the Philippines. Pay on time and in writing; in a country without a freelancer protection law, your reputation for paying is what attracts the best independent people.

### For recruiters

Read contractor CVs differently. Overlapping roles — an anchor contract, teaching, an advisory engagement — are a sign of a functioning independent practice, not job-hopping. Ask what was delivered and who operates it now, not how long each engagement lasted.

## Limitations and methodology

- **The calculation is illustrative.** The salary, business costs, 2% payment friction, billable days and 20% risk margin are my assumptions, chosen to be plausible for a senior Filipino tech professional. They are not my compensation or anyone else's. Your break-even will differ.
- **Rules are summarised and change.** Contribution rates are as published for 2025 (SSS, PhilHealth) and from February 2024 (Pag-IBIG); tax rates are as summarised by PwC (reviewed July 2026). Check the BIR, SSS, PhilHealth and Pag-IBIG directly, and use an accountant. This article is not legal or tax advice.
- **Legal cases are fact-specific.** *Royale Homes* and *Francisco* illustrate how courts reason; they do not decide your case.
- **The market data is uneven.** The strongest skilled-freelancer data (Upwork) is from US vendor surveys with an interest in the trend. The Philippine data (DICT, Payoneer, GCash, PIDS) is from 2021–2022 and covers all freelancers, most of them outside technical roles. There is no good public dataset on Filipino tech contractors' rates or utilisation.
- **Survivorship bias.** Freelancer surveys sample people who are still freelancing. Those who returned to employment are under-represented, which flatters the reported premiums.
- **Payment fees are point-in-time.** Exhibit 6 reflects published pages in October 2026; actual fees depend on the sender, currency and account.
- **My frameworks are mine.** The scorecard, matrix, pricing comparison and transition plan come from my own experience on both sides of the line. They are tools for thinking, not validated instruments.

## The trade, priced honestly

Independence is not a raise. It is a trade in which you sell outcomes instead of time and buy back, out of your own pocket, the protections an employer used to provide: social insurance, paid days off, a month's pay when things end, someone to review your work. Under Philippine rules the tax side of that trade is often in your favour. The rest is a question of how many days you can bill, and how long you can last when you cannot.

<Note kind="insight">
An employee sells time and receives safety as part of the price. A contractor sells outcomes and has to buy that safety back. Price it in — through your rate, your pipeline and your runway — or the freedom is borrowed.
</Note>
